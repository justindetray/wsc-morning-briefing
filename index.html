  • THE OIL CURVE STEEPENED - THE OPPOSITE OF THE TRIGGER WE NAMED. Yesterday we told you the one thing that would soften this view was Brent's forward curve flattening, because that would mean the market had stopped pricing a physical shortage. It did the reverse. The gap between the front contract and January 2027 widened from $7.11 to $7.62 overnight on two full-strip readings of the WSJ contracts grid taken twenty hours apart, and an independent curve tracker put it at $7.63. Brent itself printed a fresh 52-week high of $102.72 before easing to about $102.40, a sixth consecutive up-day, and front-month volume ran at roughly 48% of average. A steeper curve on thin volume is the market saying barrels are scarce right now, not that oil is permanently more valuable. Anyone reading the pullback from $102.72 as the curve flattening has confused the level with the shape.
  • US FORCES SANK FIVE IRANIAN TANKERS AND IRAN STRUCK BACK AT A BASE IN JORDAN. CENTCOM said it sank five Iranian oil tankers in the Gulf of Oman and near Kharg Island after two IRGC attempts on a US warship; Iran then said it attacked ten ships near the Strait of Hormuz and fired missiles at US forces at Al-Azraq base in Jordan. The IRGC's own account frames it as two US vessels plus eight tankers, the same ten hulls counted differently. We withheld the Jordan strike yesterday because it existed only as a snippet; it is now carried in a dated Al Jazeera report quoting CENTCOM directly and in wire copy on the Taipei Times front page, so we are reporting it today. Vessel traffic remains the tell: six commodity ships transited Hormuz on Tuesday against a ten-day average near twelve, though that count runs a day behind.
  • DIESEL SET ITS THIRD RECORD IN A WEEK AND THE OVERNIGHT MOVE IN GASOLINE WAS THE BIGGEST OF THE RUN. AAA's national average for diesel printed $5.9773 this morning, a fresh all-time high replacing the $5.9424 record set on Wednesday, with records also set on 3 and 4 September. Diesel is up 19.4 cents in a week, 67.8 cents in a month and $2.27 over the year, a 61% annual increase. Regular gasoline rose 5.25 cents overnight to $4.2770, the largest single-day move in the series on the page, and California diesel stands at $7.8730. This is the cleanest live read on how the oil move is reaching households and freight costs, and it lands the morning before PPI and the day before CPI. Our own first read of the page was a cached Wednesday copy; the figures above came from a cache-bypassed fetch confirmed twice.
  • THE MARKET WAS FAR WEAKER THAN THE INDEX LEVEL SUGGESTS. The S&P 500 fell 0.48% on Wednesday, but only 98 of its 500 members rose against 402 that fell - one stock up for every four down. The equal-weighted index fell 0.96%, twice the cap-weighted decline, and across all listed names roughly a third rose. Energy was the only sector with a positive median stock at +0.44%; utilities finished with zero of thirty-one members higher, the sharpest possible statement about rate sensitivity. This is the second straight session of narrowing, from 144 advancers on Tuesday to 98 on Wednesday. A market where the average stock is falling twice as fast as the index is not a calm market, however calm the headline number looks.
  • THE SEMICONDUCTOR RALLY IS REAL BUT IT IS ROTATING AWAY FROM THE AI WINNERS. The Philadelphia semiconductor index rose 0.37% on Wednesday, its second straight gain against a falling Dow, but the leadership inside it inverted: AMD rose 3.04% and Intel 1.69% while Nvidia fell 0.91% and Broadcom 1.13%. The index is being carried by the turnaround and laggard names, not the megacaps that drove it for two years. That distinction matters because a semis rally led by Nvidia is an AI-capex story, while one led by Intel and AMD is closer to a value rotation - and it changes what a client should expect their technology exposure to do next.
  • A STRONG TEN-YEAR AUCTION WAS THE ONE GENUINELY REASSURING DATAPOINT. The ten-year note reached 4.859% intraday on Wednesday, a 52-week high, before a $39bn reopening cleared at 4.834% with a 2.71 bid-to-cover against a 2.49 average, stopping through by 1.5 basis points, with indirect bidders at 79.2% and dealers left holding only 4.3% against a 10.2% average. Real money absorbed the supply at the highest yield in a year, which is why yields ended below their peak. Treasury yields still rose on the day - two-year to 4.43%, ten-year to 4.83%, thirty-year to 5.28% - and the two-to-ten spread flattened a basis point to 40. We could not obtain the TreasuryDirect results file itself, so the auction statistics rest on three agreeing secondary sources rather than the primary.

Wednesday's move came with the front end outrunning the long end again, which is what a market repricing the near-term policy path rather than long-run inflation looks like.

THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The oil story got firmer overnight, not softer. Yesterday we said the signal to watch was Brent's forward curve flattening, because that would mean traders had stopped pricing a genuine shortage of barrels available today. Instead the curve steepened, Brent set a fresh 52-week high, and diesel set its third record price in a week at $5.98 a gallon. The important point for a client is that this is a supply shock working through fuel costs, not a signal about the health of the economy or a reason to change a long-term plan. Underneath a quiet-looking 0.5% decline in the S&P 500, only 98 of 500 companies actually rose - the average stock is having a much harder time than the index suggests. Inflation figures land this morning and tomorrow, and they are the next real test.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

If a client asks you one question today it will be about petrol and diesel prices, so it is worth having the mechanism straight.

Yesterday we told you what would change our minds. We said the oil move looked like a genuine physical shortage rather than a repricing of oil's long-term value, and that the way to test it was the shape of the forward curve: if the premium of today's barrel over a barrel delivered in January 2027 began to shrink, the scarcity story was fading. Overnight that premium widened instead, from $7.11 to $7.62. Brent touched a fresh 52-week high above $102 before easing. So the test we set was run and the answer came back the same way it did yesterday, only more so.

Why that framing helps a client: a curve like this one says the market expects the disruption to be temporary. Traders are paying a large premium for oil right now and still expect it to be roughly $8 cheaper in sixteen months. That is a shortage of barrels in the water, caused by ships not moving through the Strait of Hormuz, and not a belief that energy has permanently repriced. It is genuinely painful in the near term and it is genuinely expected to pass. Both halves of that sentence are true and clients usually only hear the first.

The pain is real and measurable. Diesel's national average hit $5.9773 this morning, a third record in a week and up 61% on the year. Diesel is the freight input, so it flows into the price of nearly everything that moves by truck, with a lag of weeks to months. Regular gasoline rose more than five cents overnight, the sharpest single day of this run. Producer prices are published this morning and consumer prices tomorrow, and both cover August, which means neither will yet capture most of what has happened in the last ten days. Expect a gap between what the data says and what people feel at the pump, and expect that gap to close in the October and November releases rather than these.

Two things worth flagging beneath the surface. First, the market is narrower than it looks: the S&P 500 fell about half a percent on Wednesday, but only 98 of its 500 members rose, and the equal-weighted index fell twice as much as the headline one. When a client says the market seems fine, the average stock is not. Second, there was one clearly reassuring datapoint - the Treasury sold $39bn of ten-year notes at the highest yield in a year and demand was strong, with dealers left holding almost nothing. That is the bond market absorbing supply willingly, which is not what a funding problem looks like.

A correction to something we sent yesterday. We flagged an unusually wide spread of forecasts for tomorrow's inflation report and treated that dispersion as a signal. On re-checking, the two compilations that disagreed were published in 2024 and 2025 - they were old files surfacing as if current. The actual consensus is a single, uncontested figure: 0.4% headline and 0.2% core for the month. There is no forecaster disagreement to read into. We would rather correct that in writing than let it stand.

The steady line for clients remains the right one. Energy shocks are among the best-documented events in market history, they are painful, they resolve, and the resolution is rarely visible while it is happening. Nothing in the last twenty-four hours argues for changing a long-term allocation. What it does argue for is not being surprised when the next two inflation prints look worse than the economy actually is.
