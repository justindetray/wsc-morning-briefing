THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

Monday was the strongest US session in weeks. The S&P 500 closed up 1.49% at 7,764.70, the Nasdaq 100 up 2.83%, and the semiconductor index up 4.29%, driven by an AI bid that included AMD crossing a trillion dollars in market value and double-digit moves in Intel and Arm. The most useful thing to say about it this morning is that it did not travel. The Korean market opened up more than 2% and closed up 0.15%, trading below Monday's close along the way. Chinese indices opened higher and closed flat to slightly lower. Europe opened within a fraction of unchanged. US futures are flat as I write. And the clearest tell of all: the Asian chip complex, the epicentre of Monday's rally, is lower in US pre-market, with TSMC and SK Hynix both off more than a percent while the US megacaps are marginally higher. A rally that cannot reproduce itself in the next time zone is usually positioning being covered rather than a genuine change of view.

A word on the record headlines, because they will be quoted at you and they are only half right. Reuters called Monday a record Nasdaq close, the first since June 2. That is true of the Nasdaq Composite, and even there it only passes one of the two tests we apply: the closing level beat the old record by 0.10%, but the intraday high came up 0.02% short of the June 1 high. The Nasdaq 100 - the index the futures, the options book and the volatility complex actually track - is not at a record at all. It is still 0.58% below its June 2 close. The S&P 500 is 0.44% below its August 13 record, and the semiconductor index, after gaining 4.29% in a single session, is still 15.0% below where it was in June. The distinction matters because a client who hears 'record' assumes the recovery is complete, and in the indices that drive most portfolios it is not.

Oil did in miniature exactly what the equity market did in aggregate. Crude climbed through the Asian session on a Reuters report that a senior Iranian official said Tehran could reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days if Washington eases pressure, and by just before 8am London time Brent was $101.63 and November West Texas $93.17 - the first gain in five sessions. Then it broke. Between 8 and 11 UTC both contracts sold off hard on the heaviest volume of the day, and as I write West Texas is near $90.40 and Brent near $98.65, both lower on the day and extending the losing streak to five. I cannot point to a verified catalyst for the break and I am not going to invent one. What I can say is that the supply picture underneath is genuinely improving: Saudi exports are back above 4 million barrels a day this month after collapsing to 2.4 million in August, and Aramco loaded about 14 million barrels onto seven supertankers near Ras Tanura on Sunday. Against that, an armed group shut a valve on Libya's Sharara pipeline Monday, taking roughly 200,000 barrels a day off the market. Note also that a single 'WTI price' is ambiguous today: the October contract expires this afternoon near $96 and the November contract everyone now quotes is near $90.

The thing that actually decides this week is the Treasury auction calendar, and today is the first leg. Treasury sells $69bn of two-year notes at 1:00 PM with the two-year yield at 4.76%, its high for the year. There is a mechanical detail worth knowing: a high yield between 4.625% and 4.749% triggers an unscheduled reopening of the five-year, and the auction is sitting right on the top edge of that band. Monday's price action was a bull flattening into it - the ten-year rallied five basis points back below 5% to 4.96% while the two-year did not move at all, compressing the two-to-ten spread to 20 basis points. Five-year notes come Wednesday, seven-year Thursday. There is essentially no economic data until next week, so the auctions and the Fed speakers carry the tape. Williams and Vice Chair Jefferson both speak at the New York Fed's Treasury market conference mid-morning, which is the right venue for the question of who absorbs all this duration, and Barkin follows at one o'clock.

On the Fed itself, the direction of travel is unambiguous. Goolsbee said in London Monday that supply shocks have come more frequently and hit harder and that the road back to 2% may not be painless, and told reporters afterwards there would be no ambiguity about how the Fed would react if demand were adding to inflation. Musalem said outright that more hikes are likely needed and that 3.75-4.00% is still on the accommodative side. Reuters' pre-open wrap this morning has the market pricing a 57.6% chance of an October hike, up from roughly 50-55% Monday and 43.5% a week ago. I am quoting Reuters rather than our own CME feed deliberately: that feed advanced its reporting date to Monday without changing a single probability, which is our signature for a stale file, so the probability table is suppressed on the dashboard today rather than shown as if it were live. One other small thing worth filing away - Nasdaq volatility actually rose Monday, from 19.29 to 20.39, while the index itself gained 2.83%. Index vol bid through a rally is not normal and usually means someone is paying up for protection into an event.

So the client framing is this. Yesterday was a good day and today is a flat one, and the gap between them is the information. The two questions that decide the next fortnight are whether the market will absorb this week's duration without a concession, and whether Thursday's meeting between Trump and Xi produces anything more substantive than Sunday's talks did - which produced a proposal for an AI incident notification line, a plan to meet again in Shenzhen in two months, and explicitly no agreement to extend the tariff truce that expires November 10. Neither question is answered by a 4% day in semiconductors. The repricing of the cost of capital is still the dominant fact, it still marks down bonds already owned while raising the return on every dollar invested from here, and rebalancing into that gap is still the opportunity. What would change this view is a failed auction this week, or a collapse at Thursday's summit.
