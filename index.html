THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

You will hear that we threatened to bomb Oman overnight and that another ship was hit leaving the Persian Gulf, and your first thought will be the four dollars a gallon you paid this weekend. That cost is real — about a dollar a gallon more than last summer. But a higher gas bill and a threat to your retirement are two different conversations, and you only have the first one.

Here is what the coverage will miss. Oil is up about three percent in two days and stocks barely moved — the S&P closed half a percent lower and sits less than one percent below last week's record. That gap is the market pricing a shipping problem, not a shutdown. There is nothing for you to do today. What I am watching is tomorrow afternoon's Fed minutes, because what could actually hurt a balanced portfolio here is rates staying high, not the tankers.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The setup flipped overnight, and the flip is the story. Yesterday the striking fact was that Hormuz traffic had gone to zero over the weekend and crude fell anyway. Since then the escalation changed character: the 60-day US-Iran understanding lapsed Monday with no deal (Al Jazeera), President Trump threatened to bomb Oman — the country mediating the transit talks — in a Fox News interview (Wall Street Journal), and at 11:32 PM ET a vessel outbound through the strait was struck by a projectile, damaging its engine room and killing a crew member (UKMTO via Al Jazeera). Crude stopped shrugging. Brent settled at $90.87 and WTI at $84.50, both up roughly 2.6% on the day.

The equity reaction stayed orderly, and that matters. The S&P fell half a percent Monday, the VIX is still under 16, and Nasdaq futures are down about 1% this morning. That is a market pricing a persistent shipping tax on oil, not a closed strait. Hormuz transits did recover slightly, to 6 vessels Monday against a 10-day average of 11 (Reuters, citing Kpler) — constrained, but flowing.

The real transmission channel is not equities, it is rates. The 30-year Treasury yield is hovering near its highest level since 2002 (CNBC), Japan's 10-year hit 2.94%, a level unseen since 1996 (Reuters), and odds of a Fed hike in September rose to 36% from 33% on Friday (CME). Gold is the confirming tell: it gained 1.27% Monday to $4,425 while yields rose, which is a geopolitical and inflation hedge bid, not a bet on falling rates. Higher oil feeds inflation, inflation keeps the Fed tight, and a tight Fed repricing the long end is what actually pressures a 60/40 portfolio.

Home Depot gave the cleanest read on the domestic economy. It beat on both lines and comps rose 1.7%, but management only reaffirmed guidance rather than raising it, and the CFO described operating in "frozen housing market conditions" (CNBC). Housing is where high rates bite first.

For clients: separate the household cost of gasoline from the portfolio question, because they are not the same conversation. The portfolio risk in this story runs through interest rates and inflation, not through the Strait of Hormuz itself. Tomorrow's FOMC minutes at 2:00 PM and the Canada tariff deadline at 12:01 AM are the two dated catalysts this week; the war headlines are not, on their own, a reason to change an allocation.
