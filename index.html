  • THE NASDAQ 100 SET A GENUINE RECORD AND IT CLEARED THE OLD ONE BY EIGHT POINTS. Both of the two tests we apply agree this time, which is why we are willing to use the word: the closing level of 30,732.40 beat the June 2 record by 0.234%, and the intraday high of 30,770.63 beat the June 3 high by 0.027%. That second number is eight and a half index points on an index near 30,700, so treat the high-water mark as touched rather than broken. It is the first Nasdaq 100 record since June 2. The Composite also set one on both legs. The SP 500 remains 0.44% below its August 13 record and the semiconductor index, after leading the tape, is still 13.29% below its own June peak.
  • YESTERDAY'S AUCTION TRIPWIRE DID NOT TRIP. We flagged that a two-year auction stopping between 4.625% and 4.749% would force an unscheduled reopening of the five-year. It cleared at 4.787%, above the band, so today's 70 billion dollar five-year at 1:00 PM is the ordinary new issue and carries no extra supply. Read from Treasury's own auction record rather than a wire: bid-to-cover 2.63, indirect bidders 57.79%, direct 29.02%, dealers 13.19%. Indirects fell hard from August's 66.0% but landed almost exactly on the six-auction average of 58.6%, and the auction tailed the when-issued by 0.2 basis points against an average tail of 0.1. This was an ordinary auction at the highest two-year stop since May 2024, not a buyers' strike.

THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The S&P 500 closed Tuesday six one-hundredths of a point away from where it closed Monday. That is as close to literally unchanged as an index gets, and it is also completely misleading: underneath it the semiconductor index rose 2.06%, the Nasdaq 100 rose 0.82% to its first record close since June, and the Dow fell 0.36%. A flat index is not a quiet market - it is a market where the money is moving sideways between sectors rather than leaving. The rotation was into memory chips, with Micron up 5% and Western Digital up 3.7%. Two things to watch today: the $70bn five-year auction at 1:00 PM, and the fact that Nasdaq volatility rose while the Nasdaq hit a record, which is not the usual pairing.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The most useful number on the board this morning is a non-event. The S&P 500 closed Tuesday at 7,764.64 against 7,764.70 on Monday. That is a decline of six one-hundredths of one index point, 0.0008%, which every screen in the world will display as unchanged. A client glancing at the headline number would conclude nothing happened. Quite a lot happened. The semiconductor index gained 2.06%, the Nasdaq 100 gained 0.82%, the Nasdaq Composite gained 0.45% and the Dow Jones Industrial Average lost 0.36%. That is a spread of 2.42 percentage points between the best and worst major US index on a day the headline benchmark did not move. This is worth explaining to clients precisely because it is invisible: a flat index is not evidence of a quiet market, and it is certainly not evidence of money leaving. It is evidence of money rotating. The leadership was memory rather than logic - Micron closed up 5.00% and Western Digital up 3.67%, while Nvidia managed only 0.66%.

The Nasdaq 100 set a real record and I want to be precise about it, because we have been burned by loose record language before. We apply two tests: the closing level must beat the prior best close, and the intraday high must beat the prior best high. Both passed. The close of 30,732.40 beat the June 2 record by 0.234%, and the high of 30,770.63 beat the June 3 high by 0.027%. It is the first Nasdaq 100 record since June 2. But that second figure is eight and a half index points on an index trading near 30,700, so the honest characterisation is that the old high was touched and edged rather than decisively broken. And the record sits alongside two facts that complicate the celebration: the S&P 500 is still 0.44% below its August 13 record, and the semiconductor index - the group that actually drove Tuesday's move - is still 13.29% below its own June peak even after gaining 2.06%. A client who hears 'record' assumes the whole market has recovered. One index has.

Yesterday I flagged a mechanical tripwire on the two-year auction, so I owe you the resolution. A high yield printing between 4.625% and 4.749% would have forced an unscheduled reopening of the five-year and put unplanned supply into today's session. It cleared at 4.787%, above the band, so today's $70bn five-year at 1:00 PM is the ordinary scheduled new issue. I pulled the result from Treasury's own auction record rather than from a wire, and it reproduces the wire numbers exactly: bid-to-cover 2.63, indirect bidders 57.79%, direct 29.02%, primary dealers 13.19%. The story being told about this auction is that indirect demand collapsed, and that needs a caveat. Indirects did fall sharply from August's 66.0% - but 57.79% is almost exactly the six-auction average of 58.6%. August was the outlier, not September. The auction tailed the when-issued level by two-tenths of a basis point against an average tail of one tenth. What we actually had was an unremarkable auction at the highest two-year stop since May 2024. That is a story about the level of rates, not about anyone refusing to buy them.

The bond market's reaction says the same thing more clearly. On the day it absorbed $69bn of new two-year paper, the two-year yield fell five basis points to 4.71% while the ten-year sat perfectly still at 4.96%. That is a bull steepening, and it widens the two-to-ten spread from 20 to 25 basis points - the exact mirror of Monday's flattening. Look at the sequence: the auction stopped at 4.787%, some 7.7 basis points above where the two-year actually closed the day. The market demanded a concession at one o'clock and then took it straight back. Seven-year notes come Thursday and that is the last leg of this week's duration test. Separately, SoftBank's $10bn-plus bond sale drew more than $20bn of interest overnight, which is a reminder that government paper is not the only thing competing for the same dollars.

There is one genuine oddity on the board and it is the one I would actually trade. Nasdaq implied volatility went UP while the Nasdaq went to a record. VXN closed at 20.18 Tuesday against 19.29 last Friday, a rise of 4.61%, and over those same two sessions the Nasdaq 100 rose 3.67% to a record close. Volatility normally bleeds lower as an index grinds up, because the demand for protection falls as people become comfortable. Rising implied vol into a rising index usually means the opposite: somebody is paying up for downside protection rather than chasing the move, or dealers are being forced to pay for gamma they are short. Note that the VIX did the conventional thing over the same period and fell to 14.21, so this is specific to the Nasdaq surface rather than a market-wide risk bid. For a client the translation is simple: the options market is not as comfortable with this record as the index level implies, and protection on Nasdaq exposure is being bid rather than sold.

On the Fed, our CME feed is working properly again after yesterday's stale file, and it puts the October 28 hike at 55.4% with December cumulative at 89.6%. I am publishing the table today where yesterday I suppressed it, because four of the five meetings moved overnight and an independent reading this morning has October at 55.1%, which brackets our number. Reuters this morning simply calls it a coin toss. The direction of travel has not changed: Barkin said Tuesday that the risks to inflation outweigh the risks to maximum employment and that conditions are, if anything, firming, and Collins said she now sees an increased likelihood of scenarios where inflation stays notably above 2%. Governor Barr speaks on housing at 10:05 this morning. Elsewhere the euro zone flash composite PMI jumped to 53.1 from 52.0, its fastest in over three years and above the highest forecast in the Reuters poll, with firms reporting steeper operating costs - hawkish at the margin, and the euro fell anyway to near a two-month low.

So the client framing is this. Tuesday looked like nothing and was actually a substantial rotation, which is a good reminder that index level and market activity are different things, and that the cost of watching only the headline number is that you miss the part that matters. The record in the Nasdaq 100 is real but narrow, both in how far it cleared the old high and in how few indices came with it. The bond market absorbed a large auction at a two-year-high yield and then rallied, which is reassuring about demand at these levels. And the one thing that does not fit the calm picture is Nasdaq volatility rising into a record. What would change my view is a poor five-year auction this afternoon, or a failure at Thursday's Trump-Xi bilateral, where the tariff truce expiring November 10 is still unresolved and where markets actually trimmed their expectations overnight rather than raising them.
