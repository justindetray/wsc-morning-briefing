THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The question worth pre-empting this week is not about jobs or oil. It is why every major central bank is suddenly pointed the same direction, upward. The Federal Reserve is priced at a 57% chance of raising rates on 16 September and at exactly zero chance of cutting at any meeting through March 2027. A Reuters poll expects the European Central Bank to raise on Thursday. And this morning an adviser to Japan's prime minister - a self-described reflationist, meaning a dove - conceded the Bank of Japan will hike this month and keep going quarterly to 1.25% by January. Japan was the world's last source of nearly free money. That era is closing, and the yen just moved to a six-month high to say so.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The frame that will serve you best this week is the one your clients have not heard yet: for the first time in this cycle, all three of the world's major central banks are pointed in the same direction, and that direction is up. Most clients still carry a mental model in which the next move in interest rates is downward, because that was the story for most of the last two years. That model is now wrong by direction, and it is worth correcting deliberately rather than letting a client discover it from a headline in a bad week.

Start with the piece that moved overnight, because it is the one nobody is briefed on. Japan has been the world's supply of nearly free money for a generation - the place where you could borrow at essentially nothing and invest the proceeds anywhere else, which is the mechanism people mean when they say carry trade. This morning Takuji Aida, an economic adviser to Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi and a self-identified reflationist, said the Bank of Japan will likely raise rates in September and keep raising them once a quarter until the policy rate reaches 1.25% in January. The word reflationist matters: it means he is on the dovish side of the argument, the person you would expect to resist tightening. When the dove concedes, the debate is over. The yen moved immediately - USD/JPY fell 0.97% to 154.71, a six-month low for the dollar against the yen, and the euro and Australian dollar fell a comparable amount against it.

Now set the other two beside it. Futures put the odds that the Federal Reserve RAISES rates on 16 September at roughly 57%, and - this is the number worth saying out loud to a client - the probability of a cut is exactly zero at every meeting priced through March 2027. Not low. Zero. The distribution contains no outcome below the current 3.50-3.75% target band. Separately, a Reuters poll published 3 September expects the European Central Bank to raise rates this Thursday for the second and final time in what would be its shortest tightening campaign in fifteen years. Treat that one as a forecast rather than a fact - the ECB has not decided - but the direction of the expectation is the point.

Why this is genuinely useful rather than merely interesting: the price of money is the discount rate underneath every asset a client owns, and for fifteen years the marginal supplier of cheap money was Japan. If that supply is being withdrawn at the same time the Fed and the ECB are tightening, then the assumption that a market wobble will be met with easier policy - the assumption that has been correct since roughly 2009 - is not available right now. That is not a reason to sell anything. It is a reason to make sure the plan you built with a client does not quietly depend on rescue.

The supply-side story sits underneath all of it and explains why the tightening is happening into visible weakness. Diesel set another high this morning: AAA's national average is $5.9015 a gallon, and AAA's own highest-recorded-average-price field now carries that exact figure dated 9/7/26, superseding the $5.8819 of two days ago. A year ago diesel averaged $3.7088 - a rise of 59.1%. Regular gasoline is $4.1505 against $3.1971 last September, up 29.8%, though regular is nowhere near its own high of $5.0165 from June 2022. Diesel matters more than gasoline even though clients notice gasoline first, because diesel moves freight and freight is embedded in the delivered price of nearly everything. And it is rising for a reason no central bank can fix: OPEC+ stopped adding barrels this weekend, and traffic through the Strait of Hormuz has fallen to ten ships a day, the thinnest since May. Higher interest rates do not produce more diesel. If a client asks whether the Fed can do something about fuel prices, the accurate answer is: not quickly, and not directly. What it is defending is the expectation that inflation returns to target, which is why it may tighten into an energy shock even though that feels backwards.

The practical counsel is patience about timing rather than any change in allocation. US markets have now been shut for three days. Everything above - the yen, OPEC+, the German election, the Kyiv talks, two days of Hormuz disruption - has had no US venue in which to be priced, and Tuesday's open is also the moment Canada's counter-tariffs on C$27.6bn of American goods take effect at 12:01 AM. That is a recipe for a session that looks more dramatic than it is, because it will compress three days of repricing into one. Then Friday brings the August CPI report, which the Fed has effectively named as the deciding input for its September vote, with the Committee already in blackout and unable to respond to it. Expect the noise, decline to trade it, and remember that one volatile session in a holiday-shortened week is not information about a long-term plan.
