  • IRAN / OIL - THE MOST TRADEABLE DEVELOPMENT OF THE WEEKEND. US forces struck three Iranian crude oil tankers on Saturday September 5 after Iran launched ballistic missiles at two US Navy warships; an aircraft carrier and a guided-missile destroyer evaded the attacks and no US personnel were injured. CENTCOM said it would, if necessary, destroy Iran's limited and exposed oil fleet. One crude carrier was permanently disabled. The significance is the change of target class: Iran's earlier maritime attacks hit commercial shipping, whereas this is the United States striking Iran's own state export fleet and threatening the rest of it. That is a supply-side escalation rather than a sentiment one, and it happened after Friday's oil settles were struck, so neither the WTI nor the Brent tile above reflects it. Confirmed by AP copy carried at notus.org, by BBC News timestamped 14:35 GMT September 5, and by a Reuters live file. IRGC counter-claims - that it targeted three tankers transiting Hormuz without approval, and struck a US drone boat on September 6 - are single-sourced and unverified.

THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

If a client asks you one thing this week it will not be about payrolls. It will be why everything costs more when the news says the economy is strong. You now have the cleanest possible answer, and it is sitting on AAA's own website. Diesel set an all-time record on Saturday at $5.8819 a gallon, up 58.4% from $3.7123 a year ago. Regular gasoline is $4.1459 against $3.2046 last September, up 29.4%. Meanwhile Friday's jobs report showed average hourly earnings growing just 3.1% year-over-year. That gap - wages up 3%, diesel up 58% - is not a talking point. It is the entire reason the Federal Reserve is discussing raising interest rates in nine days rather than cutting them.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The useful frame for this week is that your clients are about to experience the inflation problem physically, on a holiday weekend, at a fuel pump, and that experience will shape how they hear everything else you tell them. AAA's national average for diesel hit $5.8819 a gallon on Saturday. That is not an estimate or a forecast: AAA's own highest-recorded-price field now reads $5.8819 set 9/5/26, so the record was made this weekend. A year ago diesel averaged $3.7123. That is a 58.4% increase. Regular gasoline is $4.1459 versus $3.2046 last September, up 29.4%, and AAA's own site headline reads Labor Day On Track to Set Record at the Pump. Diesel is the number that matters more than gasoline even though clients notice gasoline first, because diesel moves freight, and freight costs are embedded in the price of everything that arrives on a truck.

Now set that beside Friday's jobs report. The economy added 162,000 jobs in August against a consensus of 56,000, and the prior two months were revised UP by a combined 55,000 - July actually flipped from a reported loss of 23,000 jobs to a gain of 21,000. By any conventional reading that is a strong report. But average hourly earnings grew only 3.1% year-over-year, DECELERATING from 3.2%, and that is below the 3.4% pace of consumer price inflation in July. So the honest summary is: plenty of jobs, wages growing slower than prices, and the most freight-sensitive fuel in the economy at an all-time high. A client who tells you the economy does not feel as good as the headlines is not being irrational. They are describing real wages correctly.

This is also the cleanest explanation you will get all year for why the Federal Reserve is being discussed as a candidate to RAISE rates on September 16 rather than cut them. Futures put the odds of a hike at roughly 57% on Friday's cohort, and the probability of a cut at any meeting through March 2027 is exactly zero. Many clients still carry a mental model in which the Fed's next move is downward, because that was the story for most of the last two years. That model is now wrong by direction, and it is worth correcting explicitly rather than letting a client discover it from a headline. The reason is visible at the pump.

Where this gets genuinely useful for portfolio conversations is the distinction between what the Fed can and cannot fix. Saturday's escalation - US forces striking three Iranian crude tankers, with CENTCOM threatening Iran's wider oil fleet - is a supply-side event. Higher interest rates do not produce more diesel. They work by cooling demand, which is a slow and blunt instrument against a shock originating in the Strait of Hormuz. So if a client asks whether the Fed can do anything about fuel prices, the accurate answer is: not quickly, and not directly. What the Fed is actually defending is the expectation that inflation returns to target, which is why it may raise rates into an energy shock even though that feels counterintuitive.

The practical counsel for the week is patience about timing rather than any change in allocation. US markets are closed today and closed Monday for Labor Day. Three days of accumulating news - the tanker strikes, an OPEC+ meeting happening this morning, a German state election counting votes this afternoon, and whatever comes out of the envoys' meeting in Kyiv - has no US venue to be priced in until Tuesday morning, and Tuesday is also the morning Canada's counter-tariffs on C$27.6bn of American goods take effect. That is a recipe for a noisy open that will look more dramatic than it is, because it will contain three days of repricing compressed into one session. Then on Friday the August CPI report arrives, which the Fed has effectively named as the deciding input for its September vote. The right posture is to expect the noise, decline to trade it, and remember that a single volatile session in a holiday-shortened week is not information about a long-term plan.
