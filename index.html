  • THE 20-YEAR REOPENING TAILED AND STILL CLEARED. Treasury's own auction record has the $13bn 19-yr-11-mo reopening stopping at 5.4200% on a 2.57 bid-to-cover, the highest 20-year stop since the tenor was reintroduced in 2020. Dow Jones put the when-issued at 5.401% pre-auction against the 5.420% stop -- a tail of roughly 1.9bp. The composition is where the softness sits: indirect bidders took 52.47% against 62.93% at the 19 August original, direct rose to 30.68%, and primary dealers had to absorb 16.85% versus 12.49%. Cover was nonetheless a touch better than the original's 2.53, on $3bn less paper and 21.6bp more yield. Read it as price-clearing rather than buyer's strike: the long end took down a tightening Fed's supply at a sub-2bp concession, and the 10-year went 4.996% to 5.014% around the result rather than gapping. Dealers now carry the duration into the decision.
  • UK CPI REACCELERATED FOR A SECOND MONTH ON MOTOR FUELS. The ONS has August headline CPI at 3.1% y/y from 2.9%, and +0.5% m/m against +0.3% a year earlier; CPIH 3.3% from 3.1%. The mitigating detail is that core held at 2.6% and services at 3.4% -- transport, and motor fuels specifically, made the largest upward contribution. That is the Gulf energy shock arriving in a second G7 inflation print, not a broadening of domestic price pressure. It lands the day before the Bank of England, which is expected to hold at 3.75%, and on top of a labour report that beat on the ILO rate. Gilts barely blinked -- 10-year 5.346% against a 5.326% prior close.
  • EMPIRE STATE HALVED WHILE PRICES PAID HIT A FOUR-YEAR HIGH. The New York Fed's September survey fell thirteen points to 7.6, with new orders collapsing from 17.3 to 2.0 and shipments turning negative at -3.2, against an Econoday consensus of 14.1. Prices paid rose five points to 63.1, edging above the four-year high set in May, and prices received rose to 28.1. This printed at 8:30 Tuesday, so it is yesterday's data rather than overnight news, but it is the single most load-bearing number on the table this afternoon: activity halving into input costs at a four-year high is the configuration that produces a hawkish statement paired with a softer dot path, which is the combination the market is least positioned for.
  • THE SAUDI OUTAGE GOT WORSE PHYSICALLY WHILE CRUDE FELL. Argus reports Aramco has cancelled or deferred term crude to European refiners loading in the last ten days of September, pushing some cargoes as far as November, with at least three refiners notified and two more expecting it; Reuters has loadings suspended at Yanbu, from which no Saudi crude has departed since 11 September, and Orlen has issued at least eight spot tenders. Aramco declined to comment, and there is still no Saudi Energy Ministry statement or restart timeline. Flat price nonetheless went the other way overnight -- WTI and Brent both gave back roughly 1-2% of Tuesday's spike on reported US inventory builds. Either the paper market is discounting a restart it cannot evidence, or a build is temporarily masking a tightening physical balance. For scale: Aramco restored this line to full capacity in four days after the April strike, so five days of multi-week silence argues materially worse damage.
  • DIESEL SET ANOTHER RECORD; GASOLINE DID NOT. AAA's page, datestamped 9/16/26, has the national diesel average at $6.3103, up from $6.2694 yesterday and $5.4460 a month ago -- +70.5% year over year, and a level that matches AAA's own highest-recorded-average table exactly. Regular gasoline at $4.3672 is up 37.1% y/y but remains $0.65, or 12.9%, below the $5.0165 all-time high, so it is explicitly not a record. Diesel is the cleaner transmission channel anyway: it is a freight input, not a discretionary purchase, and it feeds the same prices-paid index that just hit a four-year high in the Empire State survey. Motor fuels were also the largest single contributor to the UK CPI acceleration. One shock, two continents, one mechanism.
  • BOND YIELDS DISPLACED THE AI BUBBLE AS THE TOP TAIL RISK. BofA's September global fund manager survey, out Tuesday, has a disorderly rise in bond yields as the number-one tail risk at roughly 33%, overtaking an AI bubble at 28%, which fell from 32% in August. The internals are the interesting part: 79% of managers expect no hyperscaler capex cut this year, up from 71%, while 42% name hyperscaler capex the most likely source of a systemic credit event and a net 33% say companies are overinvesting. Long global semiconductors is still the most crowded trade at 53%. The AI trade is being repriced through the rates channel, not the earnings channel -- which is exactly what Monday's 5.9% semiconductor drawdown and Tuesday's 0.4% bounce look like from the outside.
  • ASIA BOUGHT THE FED; WASHINGTON KILLED THE CRYPTO BILL. Every major Asian index closed higher despite Wall Street's sixth down day in seven, with Korea's 1.37% the standout -- a full recovery of Tuesday's semis-led drop, and all of it accumulated intraday after the index opened down 0.24%. Chinese gains were attributed to signs of progress in trade talks. In Washington the Clarity Act failed to advance in the Senate, 50-49, taking Coinbase down 10% and Circle down 11% in Tuesday's session; bitcoin itself is roughly unchanged. Also overnight: Anthropic signed its first Australian data-centre deal, at the $32bn Western Downs hub in Queensland, with a power draw comparable to 1.5 million households -- a hard capex commitment from the firm whose chief executive led the slow-down chorus that broke semis on Monday.
  • WHAT DID NOT HAPPEN. The blackout held -- no Fed leak, curtain-raiser or apparent official comment surfaced anywhere in the window, which is a clean negative rather than a coverage gap. There was no new attack on the East-West pipeline, no new UKMTO advisory, no new PGSA blacklist update and no new verified Houthi maritime action. Ottawa has now gone two full business days without a federal, provincial, industry or legal response to Monday's Section 338 scope change, and USD/CAD at 1.3930 shows no tariff premium; the CFIB tracker showing lines removed as well as added supports the reading that the change was narrow. And no new on-the-record Amodei, Musk or Altman statement followed the 12-14 September exchange that caused the damage.
THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The Fed is expected to raise rates this afternoon -- the first hike of this cycle -- and the market has had that priced for weeks, so the decision itself is not the event. The event is the dot plot at 2 p.m. and what the Chair says at 2:30. What actually matters for your portfolio is that the ten-year Treasury now yields 5%, and that is the number repricing everything else, including technology stocks. Yesterday's twenty-year auction is the reassuring part of the story: the government sold $13bn of long bonds at the highest yield since the tenor came back, and buyers showed up. Higher yields are a headwind to stock valuations, but they are also the best risk-free income available in nearly twenty years, and we are being paid for the bond side of your account in a way we were not two years ago.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The mechanics are worth laying out, because the headline and the substance point in different directions today.

The Federal Reserve is expected to raise its policy rate by a quarter point this afternoon, to a 3.75-4.00% band. CME's FedWatch, as of last night, puts that at about 93.5%, and -- this is the part that surprises people -- the market prices a zero probability of a rate cut at any meeting through March of next year. Instead the pricing runs the other way: by December the modal outcome is another fifty basis points of tightening on top of today, and by March a cumulative seventy-five. So the question this afternoon is not whether the Fed hikes, it is how far the Committee signals it intends to go, which is what the Summary of Economic Projections -- the dot plot -- exists to answer.

The reason the Fed is tightening into a visibly slowing economy is energy. A Saudi pipeline has been out of service since 11 September, the operator has now started cancelling cargoes to European refiners, and diesel set another national record this morning at $6.31, up more than 70% from a year ago. Diesel is a freight cost, so it propagates into the price of essentially everything that moves by truck. You can see that pass-through arriving in real time: the New York Fed's regional survey yesterday showed activity halving while the prices firms pay hit a four-year high, and this morning's UK inflation print accelerated for a second month with motor fuels as the largest single contributor. That is the same shock showing up in two economies.

Which brings us to the number that is actually driving markets. The ten-year Treasury settled at exactly 5.00% yesterday -- the first 5% close of the year -- after touching 5.04% intraday, the highest since 2007. Two-year 4.67%, twenty-year 5.40%. When the risk-free rate rises, every long-duration asset has to be discounted more heavily, and the most long-duration assets in the equity market are the technology and AI-infrastructure names. That is why the S&P has now fallen in six of seven sessions and why the Nasdaq 100 sits 5.6% below its June record while semiconductors sit 24% below theirs. Note what that is not: it is not an earnings problem. BofA's September survey of global fund managers has 79% of them expecting no reduction in hyperscaler capital spending this year, up from 71% in August -- and yet those same managers just voted a disorderly rise in bond yields their number-one tail risk, displacing the AI bubble. The AI trade is being repriced through the interest-rate channel, not the profit channel. Those two things call for different responses.

The genuinely encouraging datapoint came yesterday afternoon, and it got almost no coverage. The Treasury auctioned $13bn of twenty-year bonds at 5.420%, the highest yield for that maturity since it was reintroduced in 2020. It cleared. Bid-to-cover was 2.57, slightly better than the August original, and the concession dealers demanded was under two basis points. Foreign participation did drop and dealers had to take more of the deal than usual, so it was not a strong auction -- but the distinction between a soft auction and a failed one is the whole ballgame when the concern is whether anyone will fund the government at these levels. Yesterday, they did.

What I would watch, in order: the retail sales report at 8:30 this morning, because a strong consumer number removes the last argument against a faster path; the dot plot at 2 p.m., specifically how many officials see a policy rate above 4.25% at the end of next year; and then whether the ten-year holds 5% into Friday, when the Bank of Japan is expected to raise its own rate and quarterly options expiration adds mechanical flow. What would change my view is the pipeline restarting. The energy shock is the load-bearing assumption underneath the entire hawkish repricing, and Aramco brought this same line back in four days after an attack in April. Four days, not four weeks. If that happens, diesel falls, the inflation impulse fades, and the December dots stop making sense.
