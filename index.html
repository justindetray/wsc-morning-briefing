THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Yesterday brought good news on inflation: the Fed's preferred gauge came in well below forecasts, and markets now see the next rate hike in December rather than October. Yet the 10-year Treasury yield still rose to its highest close since 2002, which tells you long rates are being driven by more than the Fed. Stocks dipped slightly, and futures are higher this morning on strong Micron results. Nothing here calls for action; yields near 5% remain an opportunity for idle cash, and Friday's jobs report is the next real test.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

If a client calls this morning, lead with the good news, then explain why it did not lower their mortgage rate.

The good news: inflation cooled more than expected in August. The Fed's preferred measure rose 3.4% from a year ago, against forecasts of 3.7%, and the core measure that strips out food and energy came in at 3.0%. Consumer spending still grew strongly, up 0.9% in the month, and second-quarter growth was revised up to 2.2%, so this is not a weakening economy. Markets responded sensibly: the chance of another Fed rate hike in October fell to about 35%, from about 70% on Monday.

The puzzle: long-term Treasury yields rose anyway. The 10-year closed at 5.29%, its highest since May 2002, and the 30-year at 5.64%. Short rates follow the Fed and eased. Long rates follow what investors demand to lend for a decade or more, and that is being pushed up by large government deficits, heavy borrowing for AI data centers, rising bond yields abroad, including Japan, and oil back near $100. The 10-year was the trigger we flagged at 5.25%; it is now 5.29%, and it is moving for reasons a softer Fed does not fix.

Stocks are absorbing this better than headlines suggest. The S&P 500 slipped 0.25% Wednesday and sits 1.89% below its August 13 record; Micron's strong results, with revenue well ahead of forecasts and AI memory demand described as tight through 2028, have Nasdaq futures up this morning.

For clients, the practical message is unchanged: high-quality bonds now pay real returns not seen in two decades, which is an opportunity for income and idle cash, and equity portfolios built for the long run do not need changing for a yield move. What would change the view: a 10-year close above 5.40%, a 30-year above 5.75%, or a strong payrolls report Friday that puts October hike odds back above 50%.
