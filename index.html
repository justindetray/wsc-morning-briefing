  • THE BANK OF JAPAN RAISED ITS POLICY RATE TO AROUND 1.25 PERCENT, THE HIGHEST IN JAPAN SINCE 1995. The vote was 7-2 and both dissents were dovish — Asada and Sato each preferred no change at all. On a separate vote, Takata and Tamura opposed the statement's description of the price outlook, arguing underlying inflation has already reached the 2 percent target. So the board is split on both sides of centre at once. There was no Outlook Report at this meeting and no change to the JGB taper. Governor Ueda said the policy phase has shifted and declined to rule out consecutive hikes. The yen weakened anyway: USD/JPY rose about 1.2 percent to roughly 157.8, EUR/JPY 1.2 percent and AUD/JPY 1.5 percent. A hike that was already 97 percent expected by economists cannot tighten anything; only the path can, and the path disappointed.
  • CRUDE FELL FOR A THIRD CONSECUTIVE SESSION ON HOPES THE SAUDI OUTAGE STAYS CONTAINED. WTI is back below 100 dollars and Brent near 103, which puts Brent on track for its first weekly decline in three. The optimism rests on thin evidence. Reuters now reports three pumping stations on the East-West line were damaged, one more than first assessed, Saudi and Houthi forces exchanged fresh cross-border strikes overnight, and there has been no new official Saudi production figure. Regional officials earlier said repairs could take three to five weeks; the tape is instead trading single-source reporting that a bypass is days away.
  • AAA DIESEL SET A NEW ALL-TIME RECORD AT 6.4476 DOLLARS A GALLON. This is read directly off AAA's own highest-recorded-average table, which now carries today's date — not inferred from the price level. It is the third consecutive record print, from 6.3103 on Tuesday through 6.3956 on Thursday, and it is up roughly 18 percent on the month. Regular unleaded is not a record and remains about 55 cents below its June 2022 high. Diesel is the freight-cost channel, so this is the piece that keeps showing up in goods prices with a lag.
  • THURSDAY'S BOND RALLY WAS ENTIRELY A REAL-RATE MOVE, WITH INFLATION EXPECTATIONS COMPLETELY UNCHANGED. The 10-year nominal fell 7bp to 4.94 percent and the 10-year TIPS yield fell 7bp to 2.61, leaving the 10-year breakeven dead flat at 233bp on both days. That happened on an afternoon when the 10-year TIPS reopening cleared at 2.653 percent, the highest 10-year real yield in nearly 18 years, on a soft 2.24 bid-to-cover. The 10-year's move above 5 percent lasted exactly one close.
  • THE THURSDAY DATA WAS PRO-INFLATION AND PRO-HIKE, WHICH IS WHY THE RALLY IS WORTH A SECOND LOOK. Jobless claims fell 10,000 to 196,000 with no sign of labour-market cracking. The Philadelphia Fed headline beat every published estimate at 37.8, but its internals were the story: prices paid up 8 points to 48.6, prices received up 14 to 31.3 and the highest since April, while employment fell 16 points to 11.8. Rising input costs with slowing hiring is the uncomfortable combination. Housing was the offset — starts 1,275,000 and permits 1,394,000 both fell, and completions dropped 11.9 percent on the month and 27.1 percent on the year.
  • TODAY IS QUARTERLY QUAD WITCHING, THE LARGEST SINGLE EXPIRATION OF THE QUARTER. Large expirations release the dealer positioning that has been pinning the tape, so Monday's market can behave quite differently from Friday's without anything fundamental having changed.
THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Two central banks raised interest rates this week, and both stock and bond markets went up. That is not a contradiction — it is the single most useful thing to understand about how markets price policy. A rate hike that everyone already expects does almost nothing; what moves markets is the path implied for the rates that come after it. The Fed hiked Wednesday and Japan hiked overnight, and in both cases the decision itself was fully anticipated. What changed was the outlook beyond it: the market now prices a meaningfully lower peak in rates than it did 24 hours ago, so the 10-year Treasury yield fell back below 5 percent and equities rallied hard, led by semiconductors.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

If a client asks why stocks rose after the Federal Reserve raised rates, the answer is that the hike was never the news. The Fed's 25bp move on Wednesday was universally expected, and the Bank of Japan's hike to 1.25 percent overnight was expected by 97 percent of surveyed economists. Anticipated decisions are already in the price. What is not in the price is the path, and the path moved in the market's favour this week: the probability of rates ending up at or above 4.50 percent by next March fell by about four percentage points, even as the odds of another hike in October edged slightly higher. Markets marked down the destination while marking up the next step. Lower destination is what matters for long-duration assets, which is why the 10-year Treasury yield fell seven basis points to 4.94 percent, giving back all of its brief move above 5 percent, and why the Nasdaq 100 rose 1.7 percent and semiconductors 3.1 percent.

There is a subtlety worth carrying into client conversations, because it cuts against the comfortable read. The entire bond rally was a real-rate move. The 10-year inflation-protected yield fell by exactly the same seven basis points as the nominal, which means the market's expected inflation over the next decade did not budge at all — it sat at 2.33 percent before and after. Investors did not become more confident about inflation this week; they became more confident that policy will not have to go much higher. Those are different beliefs, and the second one is more fragile than the first, particularly on a day when the Philadelphia Fed's survey showed input costs accelerating and its measure of prices charged to customers hitting a five-month high.

The more practical point for portfolios is what real yields now offer. Thursday's 10-year inflation-protected Treasury auction cleared at 2.653 percent — the highest real yield on that maturity in nearly 18 years. That is a contractually guaranteed 2.65 percent above inflation, before tax, for a decade. It reframes the whole allocation question. For most of the past 15 years the argument for taking equity risk was that safe assets paid nothing after inflation; that argument no longer holds. High-quality bonds are doing real work in a portfolio again, and for clients with defined future spending — a liability in eight years, a funding commitment, a retirement date — locking in a positive real return is now a genuine option rather than a consolation prize. That does not argue for abandoning equities. It argues that the bar equities have to clear has risen, and that diversification is finally being compensated rather than merely tolerated.

One housekeeping note for anyone watching prices today. This is quarterly quad witching, the largest options and futures expiration of the quarter, and it is landing two days after a Fed meeting with volatility sharply lower. Expirations of this size release the dealer hedging flows that tend to hold the market in a narrow range, so it is entirely normal for the following week to trade with more range than the week before it. If Monday looks choppier than Friday, that is mechanical, not a message about the economy — and the week ahead has no inflation report, no growth report and no Fed meeting to give it direction.
