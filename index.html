THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The question you will get Monday: "Did the Fed just change direction?" On Friday morning the new Chair, Kevin Warsh, gave his first Jackson Hole speech and said the quiet part out loud - inflation is the bigger worry, and the central bank owns it. The bond market repriced hard: the odds of a rate HIKE in September jumped to 59% from 38% in an afternoon. The market now expects the Fed's next move to be up, not down.

Nothing here needs a decision from you. Two things are worth knowing. This was a bond event, not a stock event - two-year yields rose 11 basis points while the S&P slipped a quarter of a percent and still finished the week higher. And the pain was concentrated: semiconductors fell 3.5% Friday, and Nvidia gave back half of Thursday's jump to finish the week up just 1.3%.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

Friday was the most consequential day of Chair Warsh's tenure so far, and the reason is worth stating precisely because the headline numbers understate it. In his first Jackson Hole keynote he said that on the price-stability side of the mandate the numbers are more concerning, that 2% is a firm, fixed target, and - the line that did the damage - that we must be confident underlying inflation is moving to our objective clearly and at sufficient speed, otherwise we have work to do. He put the blame squarely on the institution he now runs, saying the responsibility for 65 months of sustained elevated inflation sits squarely with the central bank. He noted 54% of the goods and services in the consumption basket are rising faster than 3%, against 32% before the pandemic. He said labour markets are consistent with full employment and financial conditions are not broadly restrictive, which removes both of the usual arguments against tightening. And he explicitly refused to give a reaction function, saying he stands committed to a discipline and not to a decision. Every one of those quotes is from the Fed's own transcript.

The rate market took him at his word. On our own re-derived CME payload a 25bp HIKE at the September 16 meeting is now the base case at 59.00%, up from 37.64% on Thursday, with October at 71.71%, December at 89.46% and January at 92.12%. The single most telling change is December's odds of two or more hikes, which went from 29.84% to 51.81% - the market moved from a coin-flip on one more hike to a coin-flip on two. Cut probability is exactly zero at every meeting through January 2027. Two-year yields rose 11.4bp to 4.352% while the 30-year added only 1.1bp, so the curve bear-flattened and 2s10s went to 36.5bp from 43.6bp. Gold fell 3.03%, its worst session since February, and broke its 200-day average. Bitcoin gave back about 2%. I will name a spread rather than pick a number: the wires put September's odds anywhere from 55.5% to about 60%, and 59.00% is what our own payload says.

Now the part that is genuinely odd and is the most useful observation in this note. Equities did not care in aggregate and the volatility market cared even less. The S&P fell 0.25% and the Dow was unchanged. The VIX FELL to 14.43, traded as high as 14.84 and fully round-tripped, and the Nasdaq-100 volatility index closed at 19.92 - its lowest close since January 12 on our own two-year series. Front-to-three-month VIX is still in steep contango at 0.8255. But underneath the index the damage was real and narrow: semiconductors fell 3.47%, Marvell 10.28% on soft fiscal-2028 guidance, Nvidia 4.58%. Breadth was far worse than the headline, with 1,791 advancers against 2,888 decliners and only 37% of issues higher. PayPal fell 12.71% after Stripe and Advent walked away from a roughly $50 billion approach; Gap rose 12.94% on a beat and a raised forecast. When the equity market and the rate market disagree this cleanly, the disagreement itself is the observation - and the carry crosses agree with the calm reading, since EUR/JPY and AUD/JPY were both unchanged, which is not what a stress event looks like.

For the week the scoreboard was quietly positive and thinly led: the S&P rose 0.49%, the Dow 0.53% and snapped a two-week losing streak, the Nasdaq 100 rose 0.43%, and the Russell 2000 fell 1.5%. Only four of eleven sectors finished higher. The semiconductor index fell 2.31% in the same week its largest member reported 117% data-centre growth, and Nvidia itself, one day after its third-best session in two years, finished the week up just 1.32%. Investors pulled $22.33 billion from US equity funds through Aug 26, the largest weekly outflow since March, and put $7.12 billion into bonds. Two things to watch into Monday. First, the buried release: the BLS preliminary payroll benchmark revision for March 2026 came in at MINUS 79,000, a non-event next to last year's minus 911,000 and actually below the ten-year average revision - but it was published the same minute Warsh began speaking, so no market move is cleanly attributable to it, and we are saying that rather than attributing it. Second, Friday night the President announced an agreement for the US to control 65 billion barrels of Venezuelan reserves, with no agreement or mechanics released by the White House; crude settled Friday down slightly and more than 4% lower on the week. For clients: the frame this weekend is that the Fed has stopped pretending its next move might be a cut, and the market agrees with it - which makes next Friday's jobs report, eleven days before the FOMC, the most important number on the calendar, and makes this a good moment to check that a portfolio is positioned for a higher-for-longer path rather than for the rate cuts nobody is pricing any more.
