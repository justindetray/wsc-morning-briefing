  • BRENT SPIKED 6.3% ON A PORT SEIZURE AND HAS HANDED BACK MORE THAN HALF OF IT THIS MORNING WITH NO IDENTIFIABLE CAUSE. Brent settled Thursday at $107.63, up $6.42 or 6.34%, its largest single-day rise in six weeks and its highest close since 19 May. Reuters attributed it to the biggest spike in attacks on shipping since the war began, led by the Houthi seizure of Mocha, the Red Sea port beside Bab el-Mandeb, through which roughly a tenth of global oil moves. It rallied despite a bearish EIA inventory report. Then Friday's open gapped to a contract high of 109.97 and faded about six dollars to $103.70. We ran five separate query families looking for the cause of that reversal and found none - no wire, no OPEC statement, no diplomatic headline. Volume on the fade ran at 92% of the 65-day average, so this is not a liquidation flush. Treat it as positioning into the 8:30 CPI print, and note we are telling you the cause is absent rather than inventing one.
  • THE THIRTY-YEAR TREASURY YIELD IS NOW ABOVE EVERY READING OF THE POST-REINTRODUCTION ERA. The thirty-year closed Thursday at 5.37%, the ten-year at 4.95% and the two-year at 4.56%, up nine, twelve and thirteen basis points. On our own calculation from Treasury's constant-maturity series - 6,183 observations back to 1998 - 5.37% exceeds every daily print since the bond was reintroduced, the prior post-2006 peak being 5.35% on 12 June 2007. That series was suspended between February 2002 and February 2006, so the honest public framing is highest since June 2007; the vendors saying since 2004 and since 2007 are both describing something weaker than what the data shows. The ten-year is at its highest since 25 October 2023 and the two-year since 10 July 2024. The 2s10s spread finished at 39 basis points against 40 on Wednesday.
  • PRODUCER PRICES WERE IN LINE ON THE MONTH AND A TENTH HOT ON THE YEAR, AND A THIRD OF THE GOODS INCREASE WAS DIESEL. Final demand rose 0.4% in August, matching consensus, but the year-over-year rate came in at 5.4% against 5.3% expected and 4.8% prior. Core rose 0.3% on the month and 4.7% on the year. Goods rose 1.1% with energy up 4.2% while services rose only 0.1%. The BLS release states plainly that over a third of the August increase in final demand goods traces to diesel fuel, which jumped 24.1%. Two second-order lines matter more than the headline: transportation and warehousing margins rose 2.3% with truck freight up 2.0%, and margins for fuels and lubricants retailing fell 11.3% - retailers absorbed the move rather than passing it through, which leaves room for more pass-through in the September data.
  • DIESEL BROKE SIX DOLLARS FOR THE FIRST TIME EVER WHILE GASOLINE SITS SEVENTY-TWO CENTS BELOW ITS OWN RECORD. AAA's national diesel average printed $6.0556 this morning against $5.9773 yesterday, up 7.8 cents overnight, 20.6 cents in a week, 73 cents in a month and $2.35 or 63.4% on the year. That is the fifth record in seven days and the first six-dollar handle in the history of the series. Regular gasoline rose 1.8 cents to $4.2950 and remains 72 cents below its record of $5.0165. That divergence is the entire story: this is a distillate and refining-margin shock rather than a crude shock. It is the same mechanism the PPI describes with diesel up 24.1% and the same one Lagarde named explicitly in Frankfurt, which is an unusual degree of agreement between a US price index, a European central bank and a retail fuel survey.
  • THE ECB HIKED TO 2.50% AND LAGARDE NAMED THE DIESEL CHANNEL BY NAME. The deposit rate goes to 2.50%, the main refinancing rate to 2.65% and the marginal lending rate to 2.90%, effective 16 September. The September projections put HICP at 3.0% this year, 2.5% in 2027 - revised up - and 2.1% in 2028, with core at 2.5%, 2.6% and 2.3%, and growth at 0.9%, 1.4% and 1.5%. The Governing Council said risks to inflation are to the upside and risks to growth to the downside. Lagarde noted euro-area inflation rose to 3.3% in August from 2.9%, with energy price inflation at 14.3% after 10.3% and a strong contribution from refining margins on liquid fuels. Reuters reported she called the hike a no-brainer. The two-year Bund rose six basis points after the decision.

THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Two things happened overnight that a client may ask about. Diesel passed six dollars a gallon for the first time ever, a fifth record in seven days, while regular gasoline is still seventy-two cents below its own record - which tells you this is a refining and freight-fuel problem rather than a general energy crisis. And long-term interest rates reached their highest level since 2007, with the thirty-year Treasury at 5.37%. The honest read is that a supply shock in one fuel is being passed into inflation expectations and therefore into borrowing costs, and none of that is a reason to change a long-term plan. August inflation is published at 8:30 this morning, seventy-five minutes after you read this, into a market pricing a 72% chance the Fed raises rates next week.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

If a client calls today it will be about one of two numbers: the price of diesel, or the interest rate on their next mortgage. They are the same story, and the chain between them is worth having straight.

Start with what is actually scarce. Brent crude jumped 6.3% on Thursday to $107.63 after Houthi forces seized Mocha, a port beside the strait at the mouth of the Red Sea that carries roughly a tenth of the world's seaborne oil. It rose despite a US inventory report that showed more oil in storage, which is the tell that this is about barrels being unable to move rather than barrels not existing. Overnight, with no news we could find, it gave back more than half the move to about $103.70. We looked hard for a cause and there isn't one on the wires; we would rather tell you that than invent an explanation.

Now the part clients feel. The national diesel average broke six dollars this morning at $6.0556, its fifth record in seven days and up 63% on the year. Regular gasoline rose less than two cents and sits seventy-two cents below its own all-time high. That gap is the most useful fact in this brief. Crude is the input to both, so if crude alone were the problem the two would move together. They are not. The bottleneck is in refining and specifically in distillate - the fuel that moves freight. Yesterday's producer price report said the same thing arithmetically: over a third of the entire increase in goods prices traced to diesel, which rose 24.1% in a single month. And the European Central Bank, raising rates in Frankfurt on the same afternoon, attributed euro-area energy inflation of 14.3% to a strong contribution from refining margins on liquid fuels. A US price index, a European central bank and an American retail fuel survey all describing the same mechanism on the same day is rare.

That is how it reaches a client's balance sheet. Diesel is the input to almost everything that moves by truck, with a lag of weeks to months, so it flows into consumer prices later rather than now. Bond investors do not wait for the lag - they price it immediately. The thirty-year Treasury yield closed at 5.37%, which on our own calculation from Treasury's constant-maturity data is above every reading since the bond was reintroduced; the safe public phrasing is the highest since June 2007. The ten-year is at 4.95%, the highest since October 2023. Odds of a Federal Reserve rate rise next Wednesday went from roughly a coin flip a week ago to 72% this morning, and the market now assigns no chance at all to a cut at any meeting into 2027.

One detail that argues this is not a funding problem. Treasury sold $22bn of thirty-year bonds on Thursday and the statistics were strong - it cleared 2.7 basis points below the market yield, demand covered the offering 2.61 times, and indirect bidders took 79.5%, the highest share since October 2024. Real money bought the supply willingly. It simply did so at the highest thirty-year yield in nineteen years, and yields kept climbing afterwards anyway, even after Treasury bought back $5.2bn of long-dated debt the same day. Note that one outlet headlined that same auction as poor while its own newsroom published a strong-demand story hours earlier; the statistics are unambiguous and the headline was describing the yield level, not the bidding.

Two things to keep in proportion. First, the tape is much narrower than the index level suggests: the S&P 500 fell 0.58% on Thursday but 333 of its members fell against 166 that rose, the median company is down 3.4% over the past month, and only 17% of the index trades above its twenty-day average. When a client says the market looks fine, the average stock does not. Second, Asia sold this hard overnight - Korea opened down 3.3% - and then bought most of it back, and Europe is trading higher this morning. Asia and Europe are already reading the crude reversal as risk-positive.

The steady line still holds, and it is not a platitude here. Energy supply shocks are among the best-documented events in market history: they are painful, they are passed through with a lag, and they resolve, usually invisibly while everyone is still worried. What clients should expect is that the next two inflation reports read worse than the economy actually is, because August and September data will finally capture the fuel move. The thing that would genuinely change this view is not the price of crude - it is evidence that refining capacity, rather than shipping routes, has been durably impaired, because that would make the distillate premium structural instead of temporary.
