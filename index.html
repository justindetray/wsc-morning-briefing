Overnight

Single names: LULU; GWRE; IOT; ZS.

  • Governor Christopher Waller's 8:30 speech on Thursday was the single event that turned the week, and it is worth reading what he actually said rather than the headline. His words: 'while inflation remains meaningfully above the Federal Open Market Committee's 2 percent goal, recent data suggest we are finally seeing some signs of disinflation,' and 'if this continues in the data due over the next two weeks, I would be inclined to support holding the target for the federal funds rate at its current setting.' He also gave the other side: 'if the incoming data for August show this improvement has been fleeting, then it may be appropriate to raise the policy rate when the FOMC meets on September 15 and 16.' The load-bearing sentence for today is the conditional: 'my decision on the appropriate stance of policy will be heavily influenced by what we learn about August inflation,' and separately, 'I don't expect that the employment data will deviate much from what we have been seeing.' Waller pre-committed to discounting this morning's payrolls print. That asymmetry is the most tradable fact on this page: a hot number can push hike odds up through other members of the Committee, but it cannot easily undo Waller's stated conditional hold.
  • The CME FedWatch distribution advanced to a September 3 reporting cohort and repriced dovish for a second consecutive session. The September 16 meeting now shows a 48.6% probability of a hold against a 51.4% probability of a 25 basis point HIKE - a coin flip, and a further ten-point dovish move from the 38.8/61.2 split on the prior cohort. Two sessions ago this board read 31/69. October 28 shows 35.7% hold, 50.7% for one hike and 13.7% for two. December 9 shows 15.9% hold, 42.4% one hike, 34.1% two hikes and 7.6% three. January 27 shows 12.6% hold. The single most important structural fact on this board has not changed and deserves restating to any client who still frames the year around easing: the probability of a rate CUT is exactly ZERO at every meeting through March 2027, because the distribution contains no bucket below the current 3.50 to 3.75 percent target band. The market's central case is at most one more hike followed by an extended pause. This week's argument has been about the timing and count of hikes, never their direction.
  • The August employment report lands at 8:30 this morning and the forecast dispersion is the story. Named, attributed estimates: Fifth Third at MINUS 25,000, the Reuters poll of economists at plus 56,000, a FactSet median of plus 65,000 from six contributors clustered between 50,000 and 70,000, Goldman Sachs at plus 65,000 headline with plus 40,000 private and 4.1% unemployment, Wolfe Research at plus 65,000, Credit Agricole at plus 65,000, Wells Fargo at plus 80,000, and a street range topping out near plus 125,000. That is a spread of 150,000 jobs. The prior month was a DECLINE of 23,000 with unemployment at 4.1%, participation at 61.4% and average hourly earnings up 3.2% year-on-year, and May and June were revised down by a combined 103,000. One clarification on a circulating figure: a 'Goldman plus 60,000 with 4.3% unemployment' number is a conflation - 4.3% is Goldman's year-end forecast, not its August call. This release does NOT contain the annual benchmark revision; that preliminary estimate came separately on August 28 at minus 79,000 nonfarm, and the final lands in February 2027.
  • Thursday's data was mixed to hawkish, not good, and the gap between the data and the 1% rally is the honest thing to flag. ISM services came in at 55.4 against 54.1 in July, with business activity at 61.7 and new orders at 60.9 - genuinely strong. But the employment sub-index contracted for a SECOND straight month at 47.8, and the prices index printed 72.6, its fifth reading above 70 in six months. Contracting service-sector employment alongside accelerating input prices is a stagflationary mix, and the equity market chose to trade the activity headline. The July trade deficit widened by $17.4bn in one month to $88.6bn as exports fell $6.6bn and imports rose $10.8bn - a materially negative third-quarter growth input that the tape ignored, though year-to-date the deficit is still down 29.6% on 2025. Jobless claims at 206,000 for the week ending August 29 were a non-event, up 2,000 with the prior week revised up 1,000. Second-quarter unit labour costs were revised DOWN to plus 1.2% from plus 1.3%, and manufacturing unit labour costs fell 0.3%, the first decline since 2021 - quietly the most supportive item for the disinflation case Waller was making.
  • Bitcoin rose 5.12% on Thursday to close at $81,263.99 and has given back nothing overnight, trading $81,128.99 at 07:12. The catalyst is the same Waller repricing that moved everything else, amplified by forced covering: more than $415mn of short positions were liquidated as the price took out $81,000, though venue tallies of the magnitude disagree substantially, so treat the existence of a squeeze as well supported and the size as unsettled. The timing is unambiguous - Bitcoin was near $77,900 at 8:00 in the morning and essentially the entire move happened after Waller crossed the wires. The equity read-through was the day's leadership: Robinhood was the largest gainer in the S&P 500, up nearly 16%, and Coinbase rose about 10%, though Robinhood had a separate non-crypto driver in a broker note on prediction markets. One circulating explanation for the move - that US and Iranian hostilities had paused - is contradicted by the primary record for the same morning, in which Iran struck US bases in Kuwait and the UAE, and it is not used here.

On the other side, Samsara rose 15.6% on a clean beat-and-raise, UiPath rose 13%, DocuSign beat and raised and rose about 4% after peaking near 7%, and Smith and Wesson rose 11%. Asana fell 12% and Oxford Industries 15%. The dividing line across the whole cohort is not whether results beat but what the forward guide implies, which is the signature of a market with high embedded expectations and low tolerance for deceleration.

THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The August jobs report comes out at 8:30 this morning, and the honest thing to say in advance is that nobody knows what it will say. The range of professional forecasts runs from a loss of 25,000 jobs to a gain of 125,000 - that is not a disagreement about a decimal point, it is a disagreement about whether the labour market is shrinking or growing. When the spread of expert opinion is that wide, the number is not forecastable, and a portfolio built to survive only one of those outcomes is not a portfolio. Last month's report showed a loss of 23,000 jobs, so a soft print would not be new information.

The more interesting development was yesterday. A Federal Reserve governor said he is inclined to hold rates steady this month, and markets took the odds of a September rate increase from about 61% down to about 51% - a coin flip. Stocks rose about 1%, the volatility index fell to 14, and Bitcoin jumped 5%. But read the fine print: he said his decision depends on next week's inflation data, not on this morning's jobs number. That is why we treat today as noise and next Friday's inflation report as the event that actually matters.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The August employment report arrives at 8:30 this morning, and the most useful thing to know about it is how little agreement there is about what it will show. Named professional forecasts run from a loss of 25,000 jobs (Fifth Third) to a gain of 125,000 at the top of the street range, with Wells Fargo at +80,000 and Goldman Sachs, Wolfe Research and Credit Agricole all clustered at +65,000. The Reuters poll of economists sits at +56,000. Last month's report showed payrolls FALLING by 23,000, and May and June were revised down by a combined 103,000. A spread that wide is itself the message: this is not a case where the market has a confident view that a surprise will disturb. Nobody knows, the range of honest professional opinion spans growth and contraction, and portfolios should be built to be indifferent to which number prints.

Yesterday's session is the more instructive story, and it turned on one speech. Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said that recent data show 'some signs of disinflation' and that if that continues 'I would be inclined to support holding the target for the federal funds rate at its current setting' (Federal Reserve, speech text, September 3). Markets moved immediately: the odds of a September rate increase fell from about 61% to about 51% (CME FedWatch), the S&P 500 rose 1.06%, the Dow rose 1.18%, the volatility index fell nearly 6% to 14.32, and Bitcoin rose 5.1%. But the crucial sentence is the conditional one. Waller said his decision 'will be heavily influenced by what we learn about August inflation' and added that he does not expect the employment data 'to deviate much from what we have been seeing.' In other words, the Fed governor who moved the market yesterday told you in advance that this morning's jobs number is not what he is waiting for. Next Friday's inflation report is.

Underneath the rally, yesterday's data was mixed rather than good, and it is worth being precise about that. The ISM services survey came in at 55.4, comfortably expansionary - but its employment component contracted for a second straight month at 47.8, and its prices component printed 72.6, above 70 for the fifth time in six months (Institute for Supply Management). The trade deficit widened by $17.4bn in a single month to $88.6bn, a materially negative input for third-quarter growth (Census Bureau and Bureau of Economic Analysis). Jobless claims at 206,000 were a non-event. So the picture is contracting service-sector employment alongside firmly rising prices, which is an uncomfortable combination, and the equity market chose to trade the activity headline. Separately, the corporate news was harsh where it was harsh: Lululemon fell roughly 18% overnight after cutting its full-year outlook, and Guidewire fell about 15% on guidance for the second consecutive quarter.

For clients: today is a good example of a day that feels important and mostly is not. A single monthly employment estimate, with a forecast range spanning job losses to solid gains, will be revised twice and was pre-discounted by the Fed official who moved the market yesterday. The genuinely consequential events are next week - the producer price report on Thursday, the consumer price report on Friday, and the Federal Reserve meeting on the 15th and 16th, where the market currently prices a coin flip between a hold and one more increase. It is also worth restating the structural fact that gets lost in all of this: the market prices exactly zero probability of a rate CUT at any meeting between now and March 2027. Anyone still framing this year around when cuts arrive is anchored to a story the market abandoned months ago.
