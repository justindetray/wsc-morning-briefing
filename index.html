THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The call today is some version of "if the economy is this strong, why did my account go down and why is gas going up?" Both halves are real, and they are the same story. Thursday brought the best factory survey since 2021 and stocks fell anyway, because good news now means the Fed's next move is likelier a hike than a cut. Futures price no rate cut at any meeting into next year and a 38% chance of a hike in September.

The piece that reaches your wallet is gasoline, and that traces to oil at a one-month high on the new Iran sanctions - a real household cost, not a portfolio emergency. The market is not pricing runaway inflation either: the gap between regular and inflation-protected Treasuries is an ordinary 2.3%. Nothing to do in your account today; what I am watching is Monday, when Treasury details the Iran measures, and Friday the 28th, when the Fed chair speaks at Jackson Hole.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The mechanism is the one that has governed this whole summer, and it ran cleanly on Thursday. The Philadelphia Fed's August manufacturing index came in at 47.4 against roughly 25 expected, up from 41.4 and the strongest since April 2021, and its six-month outlook component hit 73.6, the highest in 43 years (Philadelphia Fed, August 20). Initial jobless claims fell to 206,000 (Labor Department, August 20). In a normal cycle that is unambiguously good news. In this one, with the funds rate at 3.50-3.75% and three regional presidents already dissenting for a hike in July, it reads as a reason the Fed cannot ease. Futures moved accordingly: the odds of a September hike rose to 38.3% from 31% a day earlier, December cumulative hike odds are 68.5%, and the probability of a cut at any meeting through January is zero (CME FedWatch, reporting date August 20).

The long end is worth separating from the inflation narrative, because they are not the same thing. The 30-year sits at 5.25% and the 20-year at 5.24%, having given back nearly all of Wednesday's decline that followed Treasury's decision to double its long-bond buyback cap. But Thursday's $8 billion 30-year TIPS reopening cleared at a 2.973% real yield, the highest for that tenor in about 25 years, and it did so with a bid-to-cover of 2.82 against 2.75 at the prior auction, with indirect participation at a multi-year high (Treasury auction results, August 20). That is solid demand at a punishing price, not a buyers' strike. Meanwhile the spread between nominal and inflation-protected yields is roughly 2.36% at ten years and 2.27% at thirty. Investors are demanding more compensation for time and supply, not protection from an inflation spiral.

The consumer read was the day's other story, and the two halves of it point in the same direction. Walmart beat on earnings and raised full-year guidance, yet US comparable sales grew just 2.6% against about 3.7% expected, the slowest in roughly six years, and third-quarter guidance came in below consensus; the stock fell 9.15% to a 2026 low, more than 20% off its high, and management pointed to customers making trade-offs in the face of higher gas prices. Michigan's preliminary August sentiment reading was 51.0, down from 55.2 in July. Then after the close, off-price retailer Ross Stores reported earnings of $2.66 against $1.94 expected with comparable sales up 10% and raised full-year guidance, and the stock rose about 8% after hours. Households are not stopping. They are trading down, and that shows up as a share shift between retailers rather than a collapse in spending.

Overnight the same tightening impulse showed up in Japan, from the other direction. July core inflation printed 1.8% year over year, the fastest since January, with core-core at 1.9%, and overnight index swaps now imply roughly an 80% chance the Bank of Japan hikes at its September 18 meeting; super-long JGB yields rose four to six basis points on the release (MUFG Research, August 21). Asia closed mixed - Hang Seng +1.2% and KOSPI +0.9% on chip strength, Nikkei -0.3% - and Europe opened flat. Bitcoin is on track for a roughly 19% weekly gain, its best since February 2024, after the administration's push for the Clarity Act, though it remains about 38% below its October 2025 record. For clients: the discomfort here is the price of an economy that refuses to slow, and a bond market repricing what money costs rather than doubting it gets paid back - which is why the honest answer to most questions this week is that nothing in the plan needs to change.
