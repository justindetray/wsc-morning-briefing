THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The question this morning: "Nvidia just had its best day in sixteen months, so why do futures point lower?" Because one stock is not a market. Technology was the ONLY S&P sector to finish green yesterday, and more NYSE stocks fell than rose. Overnight, every other company that beat expectations AND raised its forecast was sold anyway - Marvell is down 8% after raising guidance for two straight years. A tape that punishes good news from everyone except one company is narrow, not strong.

Nothing here needs a decision from you. Two things are worth knowing. Fed Chair Warsh gives his first Jackson Hole speech at 10:00 this morning, and the options market has priced almost nothing for it - Nasdaq volatility closed at a seven-month low. Meanwhile the odds of a rate HIKE by December sit at 75%, and this week the market moved that hike LATER rather than away.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

Start with the number everybody will quote and then with the one they will skip. Nvidia closed at $227.98, up 8.74%, and we checked what that ranks against rather than repeating a wire: on our own daily bars going back two years, the last time the stock gained more in a single session was April 9, 2025. It carried the whole board - the Nasdaq 100 added 1.43%, the Philadelphia semiconductor index 2.33%, the S&P 500 0.72%. Now the number nobody leads with. Information technology was the ONLY S&P sector to close green, and on the New York Stock Exchange more stocks fell than rose. The Dow, which barely owns the AI complex, added 0.20%. So the honest description of Thursday is not a rally; it is one company's earnings report distributed across four index calculations.

The overnight tape then said something clearer, and it is the most useful thing I can hand you this week. Marvell beat and raised guidance for BOTH fiscal 2027 and fiscal 2028 - and fell 7.95% before the open. Autodesk raised and fell 5.04%. Ulta raised and fell 3.39%. Gap rose 14.1% and Affirm 9.8%, so the market has not stopped rewarding anything, but the pattern across four sessions now is that beating expectations is no longer sufficient and raising guidance is no longer sufficient either. That is what a fully-valued market looks like from the inside: good news gets paid once, at the very top of the leaderboard, and everyone else pays for their multiple. It is also why the semiconductor index still sits 18.8% below its own June record even after yesterday, a fact I would put in front of anyone who thinks the AI trade has only gone up this year.

The second thread is a genuine disagreement between two markets, and when they disagree the disagreement itself is the observation. Equity volatility went to sleep: the VIX settled 14.51, its lowest since August 14, and VXN, the Nasdaq-100 volatility index, settled 20.24, which our own two-year series says is its lowest close since January 22 - a seven-month low. The front-to-three-month VIX ratio steepened to 0.826 from 0.845, which is complacency, not caution. The rate market did the opposite. Thursday's $44 billion 7-year auction stopped at 4.512%, the highest 7-year yield at auction since December 2024, with indirect bidders taking only 60.8% against 70.1% previously - that is soft foreign demand at the highest yield in twenty months. Four Fed officials spoke hawkishly on the same day: Kansas City's Schmid called inflation still stubborn and still sticky, Cleveland's Hammack said now is the time to act, Boston's Collins said it may be appropriate to tighten soon, and Chicago's Goolsbee said everybody should be on edge. And on our own re-derived CME payload the odds of a hike by December are 75.2% while September's odds FELL from 39.6% to 37.6% - the hike moved later in the calendar, it did not go away. I will correct one thing you may read elsewhere this morning: the market is not fully priced for a December hike. It is at three-quarters.

Two dates and a caution. At 10:00 this morning Chair Warsh gives his first Jackson Hole address, and the same minute brings the BLS preliminary payroll benchmark revision - the number that was revised down by 911,000 jobs last year and reset the entire labour-market debate. With July payrolls already at minus 23,000, that release is the strongest potential argument against the hawks, and almost nobody is watching it. BofA's Mark Cabana frames the speech risk usefully: a structural rather than cyclical speech would read dovish and could push the 30-year toward 5.5%. Then a week from today, August payrolls. Outside the US, the disinflation story is getting harder rather than easier - French inflation rose to 2.7% and Spanish to 4.5%, both accelerating, and Tokyo core CPI came in at 1.8% against 1.7% expected with the Bank of Japan now expected to hike as soon as September. Oil fell more than 5% on the week, its first weekly decline in three, but Qatar's mediation in Tehran produced no reopening of the Strait of Hormuz. For clients: the useful frame today is not whether Nvidia was good - it was - but that a market where only one sector rises and every other good result gets sold is a market with no margin for error priced into it, which is precisely when position sizing and a written plan earn their keep.
