Overnight

  • THE PREMISE BEHIND FRIDAY'S OIL REVERSAL CAME APART OVER THE WEEKEND. Brent fell $3.02 to settle at $104.61 on Friday, reversing from an intraday $110.19, on one idea: that Monday's meeting in Oman begins a de-escalation of Hormuz. Across Saturday and Sunday that idea degraded on four independent fronts. Bahrain formally refused to attend, saying it will not join any collective meeting including Iran before diplomatic relations resume. Iran's president said on the record that Arab foreign ministers WILL sign an agreement in Muscat and notify the IMO; a senior Iranian official told Reuters the same day that no agreement is expected, with transit fees the blocker. Bloomberg reported Sunday morning that the meeting is still not confirmed and placed it in Salalah, not Muscat. And Sunday reporting of Iran's seven conditions says the draft would CLOSE the southern route Washington has been demanding stay open. Two officials of the same government said opposite things inside 24 hours. Oman has not confirmed the meeting and there has been no US reaction.
  • THE STRAIT ITSELF GOT WORSE, NOT BETTER - TWO VESSELS STRUCK IN THIRTY HOURS. UKMTO issued Warning 134-26 after a transiting vessel was hit by an unknown projectile in Hormuz around 23:00 UTC Saturday. Before dawn Sunday an Iranian container ship was struck off Qeshm Island with one crew member killed; wire services disagree on the wounded count, three versus four, and it is not settled whether this is the same incident as the UKMTO warning. Separately the FT reported that Washington has cut guaranteed tanker protection to two specific daily sailing slots after Iran stepped up night attacks - that story missed the window by about six hours but is operationally the most important thing said all weekend.
  • DIESEL PRINTED A SEVENTH CONSECUTIVE RECORD. AAA's national average for diesel reached $6.2040 on the 9/13 stamp, up from $6.1602, the seventh straight record and roughly 66% higher than a year ago. Regular gasoline ticked up to $4.3130 from $4.3104 and remains well below its 2022 peak. This is the single cleanest primary-source number from the window and it is the one that speaks directly to Wednesday's decision: diesel is a production input, not a discretionary purchase, so it propagates into goods prices with a lag the Fed cannot outrun.
  • THE FIRST HOUTHI PROJECTILE DAMAGE INSIDE SAUDI ARABIA. A projectile wounded two people and damaged a mosque at Al-Tuwal in the Jazan region, and the Houthi spokesman threatened larger and more intense strikes deeper inside Saudi territory. Ukraine separately struck two Russian refineries overnight, in Krasnodar and Tatarstan, and Russia hit a passenger train roughly two kilometres from the Polish border. On Bab al-Mandab the Houthi transport authority claims traffic has recovered to 98.6% of its pre-ban average at 73 vessels in 48 hours; Kpler's AIS-derived count says crossings HALVED on Friday to 15 from 30. Those cannot both describe the same Friday, and no source reconciles them. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline has NOT been verified as reopened - a prediction market prices only a 33% chance of a restart by Tuesday.
  • BITCOIN WAS THE ONLY MARKET OPEN, AND IT FELL. Crude, rates, FX and equity futures were closed for the entire window; Globex and ICE do not reopen until about 6pm ET Sunday, after this brief is delivered. Every equity, rate and commodity level on this page is Friday's. The one instrument that traded is bitcoin, and it is down 0.84% from Friday's UTC close to 76,616, having traded as low as 76,504. Thin weekend liquidity makes that a weak signal, but it is the only price evidence that exists and it leans the same way the news does. Anyone quoting you a weekend crude price is quoting a model or a stale tick - one widely-syndicated page asserted a specific Sunday oil print while simultaneously conceding oil does not trade on the weekend.
  • THE FED MEETS WEDNESDAY AND THE DEBATE IS HIKE VERSUS HOLD, NOT CUTS. The target range is 3.50-3.75% and has been since December 2025; a hike takes it to 3.75-4.00%. Three members already dissented in July in favour of a hike. Our own read of the CME FedWatch API, off the September 11 cohort, puts a 25bp hike at 87.50% on Wednesday with a hold at 12.50% and a cut at ZERO, rising to a 97.66% cumulative probability of at least one hike by December where the modal outcome is a full 50bp. The blackout held - the Board's own September calendar shows zero appearances between the 5th and the 17th, so there is no Fed signal to trade and anyone claiming one is wrong. The Bank of Japan is expected to hike to 1.25% on Friday and the yen firmed into it, with EUR/JPY down 0.71% and AUD/JPY down 0.39% on Friday.
THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Friday looked like relief: the S&P rose 0.86%, the VIX fell 11% to 15.84, and oil gave back $3. That rally rested on one assumption - that Monday's meeting in Oman starts to reopen the Strait of Hormuz. Over the weekend that assumption came apart in public: Bahrain refused to attend, Iran's president and an Iranian official briefing Reuters contradicted each other on whether anything gets signed, and Bloomberg reported Sunday the meeting is not even confirmed. Meanwhile two more vessels were struck in the strait and diesel set a seventh straight record at $6.20 a gallon. So the honest framing this morning is that the news flow deteriorated in the one direction Friday's tape was not positioned for, and we will not know what it costs until Asia opens Sunday evening. The week's real event is Wednesday: the Fed is being asked to raise rates into an oil shock it cannot influence.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

THE SETUP. Friday was a good day - the S&P 500 rose 0.86% to 7,656.98, the Nasdaq 100 added 0.91%, semis jumped 1.81%, and the VIX collapsed 11.2% to 15.84. Oil came off hard, with Brent settling at $104.61 after trading as high as $110.19 intraday. Read in isolation that tape says the worst is behind us.

It rested on a single premise. Friday's oil reversal was bought because Iran had proposed a meeting in Oman on Monday with Arab foreign ministers to agree terms for shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. A negotiated reopening of the strait is genuinely the most bullish thing that could happen to this market, so the tape paid up for the possibility.

OVER THE WEEKEND THAT PREMISE DEGRADED ON FOUR SEPARATE FRONTS. Bahrain formally announced it will not participate in any collective meeting that includes Iran until diplomatic relations are restored. Iran's president said publicly that ministers will sign an agreement in Muscat and notify the International Maritime Organization - while a senior Iranian official told Reuters the same day that no agreement is expected, with transit fees the sticking point. Bloomberg reported Sunday morning that the meeting is still not confirmed and put the venue in Salalah rather than Muscat. And Sunday reporting of Iran's seven conditions indicates the draft would close the southern shipping route that Washington has been insisting stay open. Oman itself has confirmed nothing. Two officials of the same government contradicted each other inside twenty-four hours - that is the finding, not a gap in the reporting.

AND THE STRAIT PHYSICALLY GOT WORSE. UKMTO issued a warning after a commercial vessel was struck by a projectile in Hormuz late Saturday, and an Iranian container ship was hit off Qeshm Island before dawn Sunday, killing a crew member. The FT reported that the US has cut guaranteed naval protection for tankers to two specific sailing windows a day. A Houthi projectile wounded two people and damaged a mosque inside Saudi Arabia for the first time. Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline, the line that bypasses Hormuz entirely, has not been verified as restarted, and a prediction market prices only a one-in-three chance it is running by Tuesday. Be careful with sourcing here: a story claiming the pipeline is back at full capacity is circulating and is very likely false.

WHAT TO TELL A CLIENT WHO ASKS THIS MORNING. Three things. First, nothing has been priced yet - crude, rates, currencies and equity futures were all closed for the entire weekend and do not reopen until about 6pm Eastern Sunday. Every level on a screen right now is Friday's. The only market that actually traded is bitcoin, and it drifted down about 0.8%, which is weak evidence but leans the same direction as the news. Second, the deterioration is real but qualitative. We are not looking at a new number; we are looking at a diplomatic premise coming apart. That is exactly the kind of thing that gets repriced in one gap rather than over five sessions. Third, and most important for anyone's actual financial life: this does not change a plan. It changes what we should expect to feel over the next two weeks.

THE WEEK'S REAL EVENT IS WEDNESDAY, AND IT IS NOT WHAT MOST PEOPLE THINK. The conversation about the Fed has completely inverted and a lot of commentary has not caught up. The federal funds target range is 3.50-3.75% and the question Wednesday is whether the Fed RAISES it, not whether it cuts. Three FOMC members already dissented in July in favour of a hike. August core CPI came in at +0.3% month over month against +0.2% expected, headline at +0.4% with the year-over-year rate at 3.4%, and the BLS said gasoline alone accounted for more than a third of the monthly increase. Our own read of CME pricing puts a quarter-point hike at 87.5% on Wednesday with the probability of a cut at zero, and a 97.7% cumulative chance of at least one hike by December, where the most likely cumulative outcome is a full half point. The decision comes at 2pm with a fresh dot plot, and retail sales land at 8:30 that same morning.

THE UNCOMFORTABLE PART, AND THE HONEST THING TO SAY. The Fed is being asked to tighten into a supply shock it has no ability to influence. Higher rates do not reopen the Strait of Hormuz and they do not restart a Saudi pipeline. What they do is slow demand at home to offset a price increase imposed from abroad - and they do it while consumer sentiment has already fallen to 47.8 from 51.7 and one-year inflation expectations have jumped 60 basis points to 4.6%. Diesel at a seventh consecutive record of $6.20 a gallon is the number I would keep if I could keep only one, because diesel is an input to nearly everything that moves, so it feeds goods prices with a lag no rate decision can outrun. The long end already reflects some of this: the 30-year is at 5.35%, the highest since June 2007, and the 20-year actually yields more than the 30-year, which is not a normal market. Wednesday's 20-year auction and Thursday's 10-year TIPS reopening will tell us what real money charges to fund this.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND. Three specific things, in order. One: confirmed, ministerial-level attendance at the Oman meeting including the Gulf states that matter - if Bahrain reverses or Saudi Arabia shows up at the table, Friday's rally was right and I was too cautious. Two: a verified restart of the Saudi East-West pipeline from the Ministry of Energy or Aramco, which moves roughly five million barrels a day around Hormuz and would matter more than any communique. Three: the Fed holding on Wednesday with a dot plot that shows no further hikes, which would tell us the Committee is willing to look through an oil-driven headline print. Absent those, the asymmetry into Monday's open is to the downside, and the correct posture is the boring one: stay invested, stay diversified, own some real assets, and treat the next two weeks as a source of opportunity rather than a reason to act.
