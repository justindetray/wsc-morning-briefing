Overnight

Single names: SNOW; AVGO; NTAP; HPE.

  • ADP private payrolls rose just 38,000 in August against expectations near 48,000 and a downwardly revised 46,000 in July, and this single print is the pivot on which the entire week turned. Education and health services added 45,000 while manufacturing shed 17,000, so the composition is as soft as the headline: the only sector hiring is the least cyclical one. Reuters framed it as private payroll growth slowing. The mechanism from a weak labour print to a market-wide reversal is direct and worth spelling out, because it explains why every asset moved together. A softer jobs market lowers the expected path of policy rates; lower expected policy rates lower the front end of the Treasury curve, which lowers the discount rate applied to every other asset; a lower discount rate lifts equities and gold simultaneously and weakens the dollar. That is precisely the pattern observed. What did NOT happen is any change in the underlying economy - one monthly private-sector estimate with a well-documented poor correlation to the official payrolls figure moved the September hike probability by eight percentage points. Friday's official report is the number that matters.
  • Broadcom's quarter is the most important corporate datapoint of the week and it deserves to be read carefully, because the headline numbers and the stock reaction point in opposite directions. Revenue was $29,591mn, up 86% year-on-year, against roughly $29.4bn of its own guidance. AI semiconductor revenue was $16.7bn, up 221% year-on-year and 54% sequentially, against a guide near $16bn. Non-GAAP EPS was $3.32 against consensus near $3.24. Free cash flow was a record $13.67bn, or 46% of revenue. Fourth-quarter guidance is larger again: roughly $34.8bn of revenue, up 93%, with AI semiconductor revenue of $21.7bn, up 236%. On the call, management said it has secured supply for approximately $115bn of fiscal 2027 AI revenue and $230bn of fiscal 2028, that demand exceeds even the 2027 figure, that Anthropic will be its largest custom-accelerator customer in 2027 and 2028, that OpenAI's first-generation chip shipped in the quarter, and that Meta's MTIA enters production shipment next quarter. The stock is down about 3%. The reason is a single line: fourth-quarter consolidated gross margin guided to roughly 73% against 78% a year earlier. No total backlog dollar figure was disclosed this quarter, so prior-quarter backlog framing must not be carried forward.
  • The yen is the most active instrument in global markets this morning and the move is large enough to require an explicit judgment rather than a description. Dollar-yen is down 1.76% at 155.87 from 158.66, a one-month high for the yen, having traded above 160 as recently as Tuesday. Euro-yen is down 1.45% and Aussie-yen down 1.31%. The cause is a hawkish repricing of Bank of Japan policy: Bloomberg reports the BoJ favours a 25 basis point increase, board member Takata has publicly discussed larger or consecutive hikes, and the US Treasury Secretary has voiced support for decisive Japanese action. Critically, Bank of Japan accounts confirm there was NO intervention on Wednesday, so this is private positioning, not official action - though traders are braced for intervention risk around the 160 level and into a Japanese holiday. Applying the funding-squeeze test: the crosses have moved 1.3% to 1.8%, which clears the 1.5% threshold on dollar-yen, but the confirming conditions fail. VXN is 21.07 and falling, not the 28 to 30 that a genuine carry unwind requires; equity futures are flat; and the dollar is broadly soft rather than firm, with euro-dollar and Aussie-dollar both higher. This is a yen-specific policy repricing, not a global funding event. It remains the single instrument most worth watching.
  • The CME FedWatch distribution advanced to a September 2 reporting cohort and repriced materially dovish. The September 16 meeting now shows a 38.8% probability of a hold and a 61.2% probability of a 25 basis point HIKE, against 31/69 on the prior cohort - an eight-point dovish shift in a single session, directly attributable to the ADP miss. October 28 shows 27.6% hold, 54.8% for one hike and 17.6% for two. December 9 shows 11.4% hold, 38.8% one hike, 39.4% two hikes and 10.4% three. The single most important structural fact on this board has not changed: the probability of a rate CUT is exactly zero at every meeting through March 2027, because the distribution contains no bucket below the current 3.50-3.75% target band. That is worth restating to any client who still frames the year around when cuts arrive. The market's central case is one more hike followed by an extended pause, and this week's argument has been about the timing and count of hikes, never about their direction.
  • Governor Christopher Waller speaks at 8:30 this morning on the Economic Outlook at a Reuters NEXT Newsmaker interview, and this is the most consequential scheduled event on the calendar before Friday's payrolls. The Federal Reserve Board's own September calendar lists it as a speech with a topic rather than an informal appearance, which raises its policy weight. Two features of the timing matter. First, the pre-meeting communications blackout for the September 15-16 FOMC begins Saturday September 5, making Waller the last Federal Reserve voice the market hears before the quiet period. Second, he speaks the day before the August employment report, so his characterisation of the labour market is directly tradable against tomorrow's print. Cleveland's Beth Hammack also appears at 3:00pm, but that is confirmed on the Cleveland Fed's own listing as opening remarks at a Connecting Communities webinar on worker perspectives - a community-development event, not a policy speech, and it should be treated as non-market-moving by default. Her position is already known: she told CNBC on August 27 that now is the time to act on raising rates.
  • Today's labour data lands in three pieces and one has already printed. Challenger, Gray and Christmas reported August job cut announcements of 52,881 at 5:30 this morning, up 58% from July's 33,429 but down 38% from August 2025, with hiring plans of 12,325 against 16,095 in July. Year-to-date announced cuts of 529,914 are running 41% below the 892,362 of the same period in 2025. The honest reading is a labour market that is cooling at the margin rather than cracking - announcements are up sharply month-on-month from a very low base and still far below last year. Initial jobless claims follow at 8:30 against a prior of 203,000, itself a decline of 4,000 from a revised 207,000. There is no verified published consensus for this week; the only circulating figure is an aggregator estimate of 205,000. A higher number in the low 220-thousands is circulating on secondary sites, appears in no primary or poll source, and should not be used. Continuing claims of 1,778,000 cover the week that is the reference period for tomorrow's payrolls survey, and at 1.778 million against 1.777 million heading into the July report they are essentially unchanged.
  • Three of the four companies that reported after Wednesday's close beat expectations and three of the four are trading lower, which is the most useful single observation about market positioning right now. Snowflake beat and rose 24%. NetApp beat, raised guidance, and fell 8%. Hewlett Packard Enterprise delivered record revenue up 34% and non-GAAP EPS of $1.11 against its own $0.88 to $0.93 outlook, and fell 3.9%. Broadcom beat on every line and fell 3%. The dividing line is not whether results beat but what they imply about future profitability: Snowflake raised its operating-margin outlook, while Broadcom guided gross margin down five points and NetApp's forward growth pace disappointed. A market that punishes beats accompanied by margin pressure is a market with high embedded expectations and low tolerance for mix dilution. For anyone holding concentrated AI hardware exposure, that is the risk to understand: the revenue thesis is being validated and the earnings thesis is being questioned at the same time.
  • Wednesday's remaining macro releases both landed firm and neither supported the dovish reversal, which is worth flagging as the counter-evidence. Factory orders rose 0.9% in July against a 0.6% expectation, rebounding from a revised 0.2% June decline, with new orders up $5.8bn to $663.6bn per the Census Bureau; strength was broad across transportation, machinery and metals, though technology orders fell. The Federal Reserve's Beige Book, released at 2:00pm, described economic activity as expanding modestly with data centre demand specifically cited as a driver and the outlook characterised as positive, while noting persistent cost and price pressures and restrained consumer demand. Boston's district reported activity expanding slightly with a more pessimistic outlook amid inflation concerns. So the picture Wednesday was a soft labour print alongside firm manufacturing orders and a Fed survey emphasising persistent price pressure - the market chose to trade the labour miss. Today brings a genuine puzzle: the S&P Global services flash for August was 56.8 against 54.6 in July, while the ISM services consensus is roughly 54.2 to 54.3 against a 54.1 prior. That divergence should be flagged rather than reconciled.
  • The Middle East picture improved overnight in the way that matters least to oil. The notable fact inside the window is an absence: no new US or Israeli action, and the Wall Street Journal reporting that the administration is weighing a declaration that the war is over. One genuinely new kinetic event occurred - Iran struck Kuwait early Thursday and the missile was intercepted - which is simultaneously an escalation and a demonstration that regional air defence is functioning. Against that, Kpler transit data shows six vessels through the Strait of Hormuz on Wednesday against a ten-day average near thirteen, so physical flow remains impaired at roughly half of normal regardless of the diplomatic tone. That is the reason crude is the one instrument that did not participate in the overnight risk relief: WTI is up 1.43% at $92.31 and Brent up 1.05% at $96.63. No verified war-risk insurance premium datapoint exists inside the window and none is published here rather than carrying forward a stale July figure. Brent near $96 with the VIX at 15 is the tension on this board.
  • Euro-area producer prices rose 1.6% month-on-month and 5.8% year-on-year against a 1.2% monthly expectation, a clear hawkish surprise arriving one week before an ECB meeting that markets already price as more than 95% likely to deliver a rate increase. The Governing Council meets in Berlin on September 9 and 10. European equities have essentially ignored the print - the DAX and Euro STOXX 50 are each up 0.05% and the 10-year Bund is unchanged at 3.3753%, holding just off a 15-year high - which is the signature of a market that has already fully priced the outcome and is waiting for confirmation rather than adding risk. No ECB speaker appeared inside the overnight window. The broader point for a US investor is that three major central banks are now converging on the same direction at the same time: the Federal Reserve prices a 61% chance of a September hike, the ECB is priced above 95% for next week, and the Bank of Japan is reported to favour 25 basis points. Synchronised global tightening was Tuesday's panic, and it has not actually gone away - only the market's willingness to trade it has.
THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Two days ago the market was convinced the Fed was about to raise rates again and every asset was priced for it. That story has largely unwound in 48 hours. A soft private-payrolls report Wednesday pulled the odds of a September hike down from about 69% to 61%, the volatility index fell 7%, gold turned higher and stocks posted their first green day in four. Nothing about the economy changed materially in those two days - only the market's confidence about it did. That is the ordinary rhythm of a data-dependent Fed, and it is exactly why we do not reposition portfolios around a single week's narrative.

The other headline is Broadcom, which reported AI chip revenue up 221% and told investors it has already secured supply for roughly $115 billion of AI sales next year. The stock is down about 3% anyway, because profit margins on that business are guided lower. That is the honest state of the AI trade right now: the demand is real and enormous, and the market has moved on to arguing about how profitable it will be. Both things can be true, and it is why we own the theme through diversified exposure rather than through a single name.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The most useful thing to understand about this week is how quickly a confident market narrative can reverse without any underlying change in the economy. On Tuesday, the story was a synchronised global rise in the cost of money: Japanese long-term yields at their highest since 1996, German yields at a 15-year high, the US 10-year at a one-year high, and the odds of another Federal Reserve rate increase this month sitting near 69%. Stocks had fallen three sessions running. By Wednesday afternoon, most of that had unwound. The trigger was mundane: private-sector payrolls grew by just 38,000 in August against expectations near 48,000 (ADP National Employment Report), the third consecutive soft labour reading. Softer employment lowers the expected path of interest rates, and the odds of a September hike fell to about 61% (CME FedWatch).

The reversal was visible across every asset at once, which is what makes it credible rather than noise. The volatility index fell nearly 7% and closed below where it started the panic. Gold, which had fallen three straight sessions, has now risen two and sits near $4,477. The dollar softened. The Japanese 10-year yield dropped back below 3%. US stocks posted their first gain in four sessions, led - tellingly - by the Dow and small companies rather than by technology, because lower interest rates help the ordinary economy before they help long-duration growth stocks. Overnight, the Japanese yen strengthened sharply on reports the Bank of Japan is leaning toward a rate increase of its own, and it is worth saying clearly that Japanese officials did not intervene in the currency market (Bank of Japan accounts) - this was investors repricing, not governments acting.

The corporate story is Broadcom, and it is the clearest illustration of where the AI debate has moved. The company reported quarterly revenue up 86%, artificial-intelligence chip revenue up 221% to $16.7 billion, and record free cash flow. On the earnings call management said it has already locked up manufacturing supply for roughly $115 billion of AI revenue in 2027 and $230 billion in 2028, and that Anthropic will become its largest custom-chip customer. That is not a demand problem. And yet the stock is down about 3% before the open, because gross profit margins for next quarter were guided to roughly 73% against 78% a year ago. Custom AI chips are lower-margin than the products they are displacing. The market has stopped asking whether AI revenue is real and started asking what it earns.

For clients: this week is a good, concrete example of why we do not trade headlines. A narrative that felt overwhelming on Tuesday was substantially gone by Wednesday, and an investor who had acted on it would have sold into the low and bought back higher. Two real events still lie ahead - Governor Waller speaks at 8:30 this morning, the last Federal Reserve voice before the pre-meeting quiet period, and the August employment report arrives tomorrow morning, where forecasts range remarkably widely from a loss of 25,000 jobs to a gain of 90,000. That spread is itself the message: nobody knows, the range of honest professional opinion is enormous, and portfolios should be built to be indifferent to which number prints.
