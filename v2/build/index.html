Closing at the low of the day after a 1522-point range is a capitulation pattern, not an orderly repricing. The cause is the currency: USD/JPY went from 155.54 at 17:00 Monday Tokyo time to 153.77 by 10:00 Tuesday, with our own tick data showing a 152.881 low, the strongest yen since roughly late February. Tokyo Prime breadth was 362 advancers against 1158 decliners, and the damage was concentrated exactly where a currency story predicts: transportation equipment minus 4.04%, precision instruments minus 3.64%, electric appliances minus 2.76%, with JTEKT minus 8.68%, its worst day since August 2024. The breadth and sector figures come from an aggregator rather than an exchange feed and are flagged as single-sourced, though its index close matches the wires exactly. SoftBank Group was the conspicuous exception, up 5.45%.
  • THE CONTAGION TRADE, TESTED AND FALSIFIED. The available narrative this morning is that a yen surge unwound carry trades and dragged US futures lower. We tested it rather than repeating it, and it does not survive. Across the overnight window the correlation between hourly USD/JPY changes and hourly Nasdaq futures changes was minus 0.112 over twelve observations - indistinguishable from zero. The timing is worse for the story than the correlation: USD/JPY bottomed at 152.881 between 02:00 and 03:00 UTC, while Nasdaq futures bottomed at 29481.75 at 08:00 UTC, six hours later. In the window when the yen was at its strongest, from 00:00 to 04:00 UTC, Nasdaq futures RALLIED 0.37%. They only fell later, from 04:00 to 08:00 UTC, while the yen was WEAKENING. And the instrument that exists to express carry unwind, AUD/JPY, is down 0.05%. The correct reading is that Japanese equities are genuinely yen-sensitive - that link is real and well documented - and US equity futures, in this window, were not. Report the two moves; do not connect them.
  • OIL - FOURTH UP DAY, AND A PHYSICAL HIT ON SAUDI REFINING. WTI is 93.98 and Brent 98.65, with Brent having set a six-week high on Monday and now up a third consecutive session. Two caveats a desk should carry - the pre-war baseline itself is disputed, with one wire saying 125 a day and another saying 100, and Kpler and S&P Global publish materially different Hormuz counts, so any transit number must travel with its vendor's name attached.

THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Three days of news are about to hit a market that has not been open to absorb any of it. The honest framing for a client this morning is that the scariest-looking number overnight - Japan down 1.7% - is the one least likely to matter to a US portfolio, because it was caused by a Japanese interest-rate story and the instruments that would carry it abroad did not move. What deserves attention instead is quieter and more durable: three central banks on three continents are now expected to raise rates, and gasoline and diesel are grinding higher on a physical supply story that no monetary policy can fix. Those are the things that show up in a client's life and in a bond allocation.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The temptation this morning is to lead with Japan, because down 1.7% is the biggest number on the page. Resist it. Tokyo fell because the yen appreciated 1.3% - from about 155.5 late Monday to a 152.88 low - and a stronger yen mechanically compresses the earnings of the exporters that dominate the index. Transportation equipment fell 4.04% and JTEKT fell 8.68%. That is a currency-translation story about Japanese companies, and it is entirely rational for Japanese equities to fall on it. The question a client actually needs answered is whether it says anything about their own portfolio, and the evidence this morning says no. If a yen surge were unwinding global carry trades, AUD/JPY would be sliding and US futures would be following; AUD/JPY is down 0.05% and US futures are down about a seventh of a percent. We tested the link directly rather than asserting it: across the overnight window the correlation between hourly USD/JPY moves and hourly Nasdaq futures moves was minus 0.11, effectively nothing, and the two markets did not even bottom together - the yen hit its extreme six hours before Nasdaq futures hit theirs, and futures actually RALLIED while the yen was strongest. The contagion story is available, quotable, and wrong.

What is worth a client's attention is the thing no one is putting in a headline: the world's central banks have quietly reversed direction, all at once. Futures now price a 57% chance the Federal Reserve HIKES next week and assign zero probability to a cut at any meeting through March. The ECB meets Thursday, having already raised rates in June. The Bank of Japan meets the following week. Australia's market has moved from under 10% odds of a hike a month ago to 67% now. For a decade the reflex answer to any equity wobble was that the central bank would come to the rescue; that put has been sold. It does not mean sell equities - it means the diversifier that worked from 2009 to 2021 is not the diversifier that works now, and a client whose bond allocation is built for rate cuts owns the wrong bonds.

The second durable story is at the pump, and it is the one clients will raise unprompted. Diesel's national average is 5.9012 dollars a gallon, three hundredths of a cent below the all-time record it set YESTERDAY, and it is up 2.20 dollars in a year. Regular is 4.1514, up 95 cents year-over-year but still 17% below its own 2022 record. Diesel is the freight input, so it propagates into goods prices with a lag of months, and it is rising because of physical supply disruption - a fourth straight up day in crude, the Jizan refinery struck for the third time in two months, Hormuz transits running at roughly 10 vessels a day against about 125 before the war. That is a genuine inflation impulse arriving from the supply side into a Fed that is already leaning toward hiking, which is precisely the combination that makes the coming CPI print on Friday matter more than usual. Note carefully that we have no verified consensus estimate for either Thursday's PPI or Friday's CPI - none was published by any primary wire we could reach - so anyone quoting a forecast to you this week is quoting a vendor's model, not a poll.

The practical counsel is unchanged and worth saying plainly: today's open will look noisy because it is absorbing three days of accumulated news in one print, and noise at an open after a long weekend is not information. The two things actually worth repositioning around are the rate-hiking regime and the supply-side energy impulse, and neither of them requires acting before lunch.
