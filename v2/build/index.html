  • HORMUZ, CONFIRMED FATALITIES AND A NEW US DOCTRINE. Saudi Arabia's national carrier Bahri confirmed on Wednesday that two Filipino seafarers were killed aboard its vessel SIDR, IMO 9854715, in a security incident while transiting the Strait of Hormuz at approximately 11:40 p.m. local time on August 31. That upgrades what was a UKMTO advisory report into an owner-confirmed attack with loss of life. Separately and more consequentially for oil, the United States has announced a 'tanker for tanker' deterrence policy - striking Iranian oil tankers in response to attacks on commercial shipping - and Axios reported that two Iranian government tankers were hit in Tuesday's action. Note carefully what is NOT established: Bahri itself used the neutral phrase 'security incident' and did not assign blame, and no official body has produced a forensic attribution of the SIDR strike to Iran. CENTCOM's 'attempted Iranian attacks' language is a US characterisation, not an attribution. The 'tanker for tanker' policy has been reported by multiple outlets but does not yet appear on a CENTCOM or Defense Department primary release. The escalation vector that matters for markets is that Iranian EXPORT barrels, not merely transit barrels, are now declared targets.
  • THE PHYSICAL DISRUPTION IS REAL AND MEASURABLE, WHICH IS WHY THE FLAT OIL PRICE IS SO STRANGE. Kpler counted FOUR commodity vessels transiting the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday - one VLCC, one Panamax tanker, one Kamsarmax and one intermediate tanker - down from ten the prior day and against a ten-day average near thirteen. That is a roughly 70% collapse in transit volume through a waterway carrying close to a fifth of global seaborne crude, and it is a hard count rather than a sentiment read. Set that against the price action: WTI rose about 5.7% Tuesday, traded up to 92.28 overnight on the fatality confirmation, and closed the loop back at 90.21 - flat. Brent is up 0.33% at 94.96 after printing above 95, a six-week high. Two honest gaps worth stating: no war-risk insurance premium quote could be verified in the overnight window, so any specific premium number circulating today is unsourced - the most recent verified data is from July, at 7.5-10% of hull value per Marsh. And the formal status of the strait, open or closed or restricted, could not be verified from any official body. The Kpler transit count is the defensible framing: severely reduced, not officially closed.
  • JAPAN'S 10-YEAR YIELD IS ABOVE 3% AND HOLDING - FIRST TIME SINCE 1996. This is the overnight event with the widest reach, and it resolves a conflict this briefing flagged as unresolved yesterday. Tuesday's feeds disagreed on whether the settle was 2.947% or 3.00%; the answer is that 2.947 was Tuesday's LOW, misread as a settle, while Quick data via Morningstar had the intraday touch at 3.000% trimming to 2.995%. This morning the yield is 3.001% on Investing.com's own real-time page, having traded a 2.995-3.016 range overnight - so the 3% threshold has now been crossed AND held, which is a different and more significant fact than touching it. Governor Ueda said Tuesday the Bank of Japan 'will continue to raise interest rates as financial conditions remain accommodative' and will debate this month whether upside inflation risks are heightening. Market-implied odds of a September BOJ hike were around 85% in late August. Thirty years of Japanese yield repression functioning as the world's cheapest funding source is what is being unwound here, and it raises the global hurdle rate for every long-duration asset.
  • AND EUROPE IS NOW PRICING HIKES TOO, WHICH MAKES THIS SYNCHRONISED. The German 10-year Bund yield hit its highest level since April 2011 overnight. More telling than the level is the repricing behind it: investors now see a near-40% chance the ECB deposit facility rate reaches 3% by March 2027, up from 25% just a week ago. For reference the deposit rate currently stands at 2.25%, having been raised 25bp effective 17 June 2026 - and it is worth flagging that the 2.00% figure still circulating widely is stale by more than a year, being the level in force from June 2025 to June 2026. The ECB's Governing Council meets September 9-10, hosted by the Bundesbank in Berlin rather than in Frankfurt, with the decision and press conference on the 10th. So: the Fed at 69% for a September hike, the BOJ explicitly promising more, and the ECB now carrying a live tightening path. Three major central banks leaning the same direction simultaneously is the first occurrence of this cycle, and it is the mechanism behind every other number on this page.

Federal Reserve Governor Michael Barr spoke at 9:05 a.m. Tuesday in Washington and delivered the most explicit hawkish signal from a sitting governor this cycle. Verbatim from the Fed's own published text: 'if inflation appears not to be moderating sufficiently, then I think we should act decisively to raise rates.' But inflation remains too high - and has been for over five years' - and named the risk directly: 'With inflation above target for a protracted period, there is a risk of broader price pressures taking hold, a risk I am watching closely.' THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The story this morning is not the Middle East, even though the Middle East supplied the frightening headlines. It is that three of the world's major central banks are simultaneously priced to raise interest rates - the Federal Reserve at 69% odds for September, the Bank of Japan explicitly promising more, and the ECB now carrying a near-40% market-implied chance of a 3% deposit rate by March. That is the first time in this cycle all three have leaned the same direction at once, and it repriced the cost of money worldwide overnight. Here is the honest tell that it is a rates story and not a war story: gold fell for a third straight day, and crude spiked overnight and gave every cent of it back. Gold does not fall on war. It falls when real yields rise. For a diversified portfolio this is a multiple-compression environment, not an earnings collapse - and those are two very different kinds of drawdown.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

Let me separate the loud thing from the important thing, because this morning they are not the same.

The loud thing is the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi Arabia's national carrier Bahri confirmed that two Filipino seafarers were killed aboard its vessel SIDR, the United States has declared a 'tanker for tanker' policy of striking Iranian tankers in response to attacks on commercial shipping, and Kpler counted four commodity vessels transiting the strait on Tuesday against a ten-day average near thirteen. That is a genuine, current, physical disruption to roughly a fifth of the world's seaborne oil, and it deserves to be taken seriously.

The important thing is that markets largely looked past it. Crude rose about 5.7% Tuesday, traded up to 92.28 overnight on the confirmation of the deaths, and then round-tripped all the way back to 90.21. Gold fell for a third consecutive session. Bitcoin fell. The VIX is 16.7. None of that is what a war-panic tape looks like.

What markets are actually trading is the cost of money, and it is rising everywhere at once. Japan's 10-year government bond yield is holding above 3% for the first time since 1996, after Governor Ueda said plainly that the Bank of Japan 'will continue to raise interest rates.' Germany's 10-year Bund is at its highest since April 2011, and investors now price a near-40% chance the ECB deposit rate reaches 3% by March 2027, up from 25% a week ago. And here at home, Governor Michael Barr said on Tuesday morning that if inflation 'appears not to be moderating sufficiently, then I think we should act decisively to raise rates.' September hike odds finished Tuesday at 69% on CME's own data, up from roughly a third before Chair Warsh spoke at Jackson Hole five days ago. Barclays now expects hikes in September and December; Deutsche Bank expects the same.

So this is a global discount-rate shock, and it explains the shape of the selling precisely. Tuesday's declines lined up in order of duration: the Dow fell 0.8%, the S&P 0.7%, the Nasdaq 100 1.3%, and semiconductors 2.1%. Overnight, Samsung fell 3.3% and SK Hynix 3.5%. When the discount rate rises, the assets whose value sits furthest in the future fall the most. That is arithmetic, not sentiment.

And while we are being precise: nothing here is at a record. The S&P 500 closed Tuesday 2.1% below its August 13 record close, the Nasdaq 100 is 5.2% below its June 2 record, the Dow 2.9% below its August 5 record - and semiconductors are 22.9% below their June 22 peak, ten weeks later, even as the AI spending numbers underneath them keep accelerating. That last gap is the one I would keep in front of any client who thinks this trade only travels in one direction.

Now the part worth actually holding onto. A rising-rate shock is painful, but it is a compression of valuations, not a destruction of earnings - and those two things have very different histories. Look at what companies reported last night. Dell grew revenue 58% to a record $46.97bn, raised its full-year revenue guidance by $25bn, and disclosed a record $95bn AI-server backlog. Broadcom, which reports tonight, has guided to roughly $29.4bn of revenue, up 84% year over year, with $16bn of that from AI semiconductors - the first quarter in the company's history in which AI would be a majority of sales. Earnings are not the problem here. The price paid for those earnings is what is being renegotiated.

That distinction matters for behaviour. Drawdowns driven by multiple compression have historically been recoverable for investors who stayed invested, because the underlying cash flows kept growing through them. Drawdowns driven by earnings collapse are the ones that take years. Nothing in last night's reports looks like the second kind.

One caution I want to be straight about, because it cuts against the comfortable read. The market is pricing a rate repricing and essentially not pricing the war at all - a 16.7 VIX alongside confirmed fatalities in Hormuz and Iranian strikes on Jordan, Bahrain and Kuwait. That may prove correct. It also means that if the Middle East does escalate into sustained supply loss, very little of that outcome is currently in the price. The practical implication is not to sell anything. It is that portfolio insurance is unusually cheap relative to a risk that is visibly on the table, which is a better problem to have than the reverse.
