THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Most calls this week will start with gas, not stocks. You are paying about $4.07 a gallon, roughly a third more than last summer, and the headlines say we are about to seize the Strait of Hormuz. The gas number is a real hit to your monthly budget and I will not pretend otherwise. But a higher fuel bill and a portfolio emergency are different conversations, and you only have the first one.

Here is the tell. Not one tanker crossed Hormuz on Sunday, and oil still traded lower overnight. The market has already priced the blockade — what it is not pricing is a closure. Stocks are within a quarter percent of their record high. There is nothing for you to do today. What I am watching is Wednesday: new tariffs on Canada take effect after midnight Tuesday, and the Fed's minutes come out that afternoon. If this picture changes, it changes there — and I will call you.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The single most useful fact this morning is a divergence. Hormuz traffic has effectively stopped — five commodity vessels transited Saturday and none at all registered Sunday, against 31 the prior weekend (Reuters, citing Kpler shiptracking) — and yet crude opened the week softer, with WTI around $82 and Brent around $88, both a touch below Friday's settles. A blockade that real would normally command a risk premium. It is not getting one. The honest read is that the market has already discounted a constrained-but-flowing strait and is reserving its panic for an actual closure. That is a fragile equilibrium, not a resolved one.

Two deadlines land in the next 48 hours and neither is resolved. The US–Iran memorandum's 60-day point falls today, though I would treat the reporting with care: outlets disagree on whether it was signed June 17 or 18 and whether it expires Sunday or Monday, and Iran's foreign ministry spokesman says the text contains no 60-day provision at all. Worth repeating a correction from yesterday, because it is still circulating: the "August 21 Iran sanctions cliff" does not exist. Treasury revoked General License X on July 7 and its replacement's wind-down ended July 17 (OFAC recent actions). Separately, Treasury Secretary Bessent has promised "unprecedented" economic measures against Iran this week — nothing has been announced yet, and that is the genuine live catalyst rather than any calendar date.

On Canada, the answer to yesterday's open question is simply no. LeBlanc spent Sunday in Washington with no meetings scheduled, and the Canadian Chamber of Commerce chief said after a Sunday briefing that there is "nothing yet tabled" (POLITICO). The 50% tariff on roughly $20 billion of Canadian goods, with no USMCA exemption, is still scheduled for 12:01 a.m. Wednesday. Meanwhile China's July data missed across the board — retail sales up just 0.6% against 1.5% expected, industrial output 4.5%, fixed-asset investment down 6.7% year to date (NBS via Reuters) — and Asian equities rose anyway, which tells you how much stimulus expectation is embedded. Japan's 10-year yield touched 2.93%, a 30-year high, on a weak Q2 GDP print.

For clients: the tape is calm and the calendar is not. VIX is under 15, the S&P closed Friday 0.2% below Thursday's record, and futures are flat-to-higher — all while consumers pull back (July retail sales −0.6%), gasoline sits at its highest-ever level this late in a year, and rate futures put roughly a one-in-three chance on a Fed hike in September. None of that argues for portfolio action today. It argues for setting expectations now that this week has more headline risk than last week did, and for being clear in advance about what would actually change your plan — which, for nearly every household, is nothing that happens between Monday and Friday.
