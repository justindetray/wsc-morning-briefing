Overnight

Single names: TSLA; SNDK; AAPL; LULU.

  • The August employment report is the fact everything else this weekend hangs from, so the primary numbers first, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics release itself rather than any wire summary. Nonfarm payrolls rose 162,000. The Reuters poll consensus was 56,000; the Wall Street Journal survey had 53,000; the full street range ran from MINUS 25,000 to plus 125,000. The actual print therefore came in above the top of the entire published range, and Bloomberg reported that it beat every single estimate in its own survey. Private payrolls contributed 127,000 and government 35,000, split 41,000 goods-producing and 86,000 private services. Unemployment held at 4.1%, participation edged up to 61.6%, and the employment-population ratio was 59.1%. Average weekly hours rose a tenth to 34.4. For scale, the average monthly gain over the preceding twelve months was 31,000, so August ran at more than five times trend. This is not a close-to-consensus print being spun; it is a large upside surprise on the single series the Federal Reserve watches most closely after inflation.
  • The revisions are the part of Friday's release that deserves more attention than the headline, because they reverse a narrative rather than adjust a number. July nonfarm payrolls, originally reported as a LOSS of 23,000 jobs, were revised to a GAIN of 21,000 - a sign flip. June was revised up from 20,000 to 31,000. The BLS's own language: employment in June and July combined is 55,000 higher than previously reported. The July contraction was the empirical centre of the entire 'the labour market is cracking and the Fed must cut' argument that ran through August, and it has now been revised out of existence. Two implications follow. First, anyone whose positioning rested on that print is holding a thesis whose evidence has been withdrawn. Second, and more usefully as a general discipline, this is the third time this year that a monthly payroll figure has been materially revised in a direction that changed its meaning - which is the strongest available argument for never re-rating a portfolio on a single month of employment data.
  • The internals are softer than the headline and both facts are true at once. Average hourly earnings rose ten cents, or 0.3% on the month, to $37.75 - but the year-over-year rate DECELERATED to 3.1% from 3.2%. That makes this a volume beat rather than a wage beat, which is the single most important nuance for the inflation debate: more people working at slower wage growth is not the classic overheating signature. Note also that 3.1% wage growth sits BELOW July's 3.4% consumer inflation rate, so real average wages are still falling. The broader U-6 underemployment rate improved to 7.7% from 7.9%, driven by a 414,000 drop in people working part-time for economic reasons, to 4.4 million - genuinely good news. But the long-term unemployed remain 27.0% of all unemployed, which is elevated. And the composition is narrow: food services added 59,000 against a 12,000 twelve-month average, local government education 42,000, construction 22,000 and manufacturing 16,000, while health care contributed only 13,000 against a 32,000 average and the information sector LOST 23,000 jobs.
  • Governor Christopher Waller's Thursday speech is now the most interesting document in the file, because the data arrived one day later and did precisely what he said he would ignore. His words, from the Federal Reserve's published text of September 3: recent data 'suggest we are finally seeing some signs of disinflation,' and if that continues 'I would be inclined to support holding the target for the federal funds rate at its current setting.' Then the two conditionals that matter: his decision 'will be heavily influenced by what we learn about August inflation,' and 'I don't expect that the employment data will deviate much from what we have been seeing.' Payrolls then beat consensus by roughly a hundred thousand jobs. Waller was the last Federal Reserve voice the market hears before the communications blackout, which began TODAY, Saturday September 5, and runs to September 17. So the Committee's most explicit recent dove has pre-committed to a hold conditional on inflation, the hawkish anchor remains Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole keynote of August 28, and that disagreement now cannot be updated publicly for twelve days.
  • The rate-hike distribution repriced hawkish but by far less than the size of the data surprise would suggest, and this gap is the most tradable observation on the page. The CME FedWatch cohort advanced to a September 4 reporting date. The September 16 meeting now shows 42.6% hold against 57.4% for a 25 basis point HIKE, versus a 48.6/51.4 split on the prior cohort - so hike odds rose about six points on a print that beat by more than a hundred thousand jobs. Other estimates disperse widely and are reported as a range rather than a point: wire surveys put September hike odds at 58% to 65%, Citi at 61%, and the Polymarket prediction market nearer 51% to 53%. Bloomberg calculated that pricing for the September meeting rose only about three basis points, from 13 to 16, with 38 basis points of cumulative tightening priced by year-end. Cumulative hike probability builds to 68.7% by October 28, 84.7% by December 9 and 88.2% by January 27. And the structural fact is unchanged: the probability of a rate CUT is exactly ZERO at every meeting through March 2027, because the distribution contains no bucket below the current 3.50 to 3.75 percent band.
  • Why did such a large surprise move pricing so little? Because the practitioners quoted after the release were nearly unanimous that the decision had been handed to next Friday's inflation report. TD Securities' Molly Brooks: 'Pricing for September's meeting is not materially higher, signalling that markets will be waiting on next week's CPI print.' BlackRock's Rick Rieder team, via Jeffrey Rosenberg: 'A September rate hike is all conditional on that inflation report. If inflation is continuing to show progress, then it's a hold.' WisdomTree's Kevin Flanagan: 'the labour data is not any type of impediment to a rate hike. Inflation data takes centre stage.' Against that, Capital Economics was blunt in the other direction: 'Even the most committed dove would struggle to find anything in the August employment report to justify keeping interest rates unchanged.' Pantheon called the September meeting 'finely balanced.' Two forecast changes are worth recording as evidence of how far the dovish case has been abandoned: Citigroup pushed its next-cut call from October 2026 all the way to June 2027, and Nationwide's Kathy Bostjancic now expects two 25 basis point HIKES by year-end, taking the funds rate to 4 to 4.25 percent.
  • The Treasury curve told the hawkish story more clearly than equities did, and the intraday extremes matter because the closing levels understate the shock. On the official 3:30 PM constant-maturity fixes the 2-year rose 3 basis points to 4.37% and the 10-year 1 basis point to 4.78%, flattening 2s10s to 41 basis points from 43 - bear flattening, the textbook response to hawkish data. But intraday, newswire prints put the 2-year at 4.416%, its highest level since January 2025 and roughly 8 basis points up on the session, with the 5-year at 4.58%, also a January-2025 high, and the 10-year as high as 4.802%. Crucially, yields had been FALLING before 8:30 and reversed sharply on the release, so the move is unambiguously attributable to the data. The 30-year actually fell a basis point to 5.24%, which is the shape that matters: this was a policy-path repricing, not an inflation-expectations or term-premium event. The dollar index rose 0.25% to 99.16 in confirmation. Bloomberg separately flags a post-Labor-Day corporate issuance deluge plus next week's auctions as supply overhang, and Norway's sovereign wealth fund has proposed cutting government bonds, notably Treasuries, to 50% of its bond portfolio from 70%.
  • Equities produced the most striking dispersion of the week and the index levels conceal all of it. The Philadelphia semiconductor index rose 3.375% with ALL 30 constituents higher, while the S&P 500 fell 0.376% and the Dow 0.506%. The cause was a memory and storage up-cycle bid rather than the familiar AI-GPU trade, and the arithmetic is the proof: NVIDIA rose just 0.84% and Broadcom 0.21%, so the mega-caps were laggards and the index move came from the equipment and mid-cap cohort - KLA +7.32%, Marvell +7.05%, AMD +4.69%, Intel +4.51%, Applied Materials +4.31%, ASML +4.17%. Deliberate caveat: no cap-weighted contribution decomposition was available this run, so no name here is credited with driving the index move - each is reported strictly as a largest reported percentage gainer. The proximate catalyst is a Yonhap report that Micron plans to roughly double monthly high-bandwidth-memory capacity to about 100,000 wafers by the end of 2026, from roughly 40,000 to 50,000, for 12-layer HBM4 destined for NVIDIA's Vera Rubin platform. That report reached this desk only through an AI-translated aggregator and the primary Yonhap piece was NOT located, so it is single-sourced and flagged as such.
  • The offsetting drags were single-name and severe, which is why the index did almost nothing. Tesla fell 5.92% to $354.08 after an invite-only, non-livestreamed Cybercab robotaxi event in Austin that Elon Musk did not attend, and the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration opened an investigation into Cybercab certification the same day. Apple fell 2.51% on a Nikkei report that foldable iPhone output is running at only a few hundred units a day, compounded by the memory-cost pressure that was simultaneously lifting the storage complex - a clean illustration of margin transferring from device assemblers to component makers. Microsoft fell 2.04%, Alphabet 1.11%, and Meta rose 1.00%. Away from technology, Lululemon fell a further 17.19% after Thursday's guidance cut and Fair Isaac fell 15.91%. NVIDIA also closed its acquisition of Hugging Face on Friday, at $12.9bn, comprising $11.9bn to stockholders and up to $1bn of retention. Barron's summarised the session better than any of this: the three major indices all fell slightly, and the jobs report was not really why.
  • Three live weekend items, and one of them is happening as this is written. First, Steve Witkoff and Jared Kushner meet Vladimir Putin at the Kremlin TODAY, Saturday September 5, and Volodymyr Zelensky says he will meet them in Ukraine on Sunday after Moscow - the pair's first-ever Ukraine leg - and has offered to guarantee the safety of Ukrainian airspace for the visit. This follows CIA Director John Ratcliffe's meeting with Russian intelligence chiefs in Moscow ten days ago. The counter-signal inside the same window: a Russian attack on Kamianske in Dnipropetrovsk killed four people and injured five. Second, President Trump escalated on both the Federal Reserve and trade on Friday, writing that 'We should have the LOWEST RATE of any country in the World' and 'LOWER THE RATE OR I'LL STOP TRADING WITH COUNTRIES WITH WHICH WE HAVE A DEFICIT,' adding that 'The Fed Board, with its great new leader, must get smart - BE PATRIOTS for a change.' Third, and most directly relevant to next Friday's inflation print, the AAA national average for diesel set a record at $5.85 a gallon, against $3.71 a year ago. Freight costs pass into goods prices with a lag, and that is the specific channel through which the August CPI carries upside risk.
THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

If you read the weekend papers you will see that the American economy added 162,000 jobs in August when forecasters expected something closer to 56,000, and that markets responded by raising the odds the Federal Reserve raises interest rates again in ten days. That is good news being reported as bad news, and the first job is separating the part that touches your household from the part that touches your portfolio. A strong labour market is not a portfolio emergency - the cost of living is the genuinely expensive thing right now, and that is a budgeting question rather than a reason to change how you are invested. Diesel just set a record above five dollars and eighty cents a gallon, and that is real money.

Here is the fact that should lower the temperature. On the day of that supposedly shocking jobs report, the S&P 500 fell about a third of one percent, and it finished the week almost exactly where it started. It sits roughly 1% below its all-time high. The measure of how frightened the options market is closed under 15, near the low end of its range for the last six weeks. There is nothing to do this weekend. The date worth putting in the calendar is Friday, September 11, when the August inflation report arrives - the Fed has said publicly that inflation, not jobs, decides this month's vote, and Fed officials go silent from today, so there will be no official commentary between now and then.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The August employment report, released Friday morning, showed nonfarm payrolls rising 162,000. The Reuters poll of economists had 56,000; the Wall Street Journal's survey had 53,000; the full range of street forecasts ran from a loss of 25,000 to a gain of 125,000. The actual number came in above the top of that entire range, and Bloomberg reported that it beat every estimate in its own survey. For scale, the average monthly gain over the preceding twelve months was 31,000, so August ran at more than five times trend (Bureau of Labor Statistics, Employment Situation, USDL-26-1435).

Two revisions matter more than the headline. July, originally reported as a LOSS of 23,000 jobs - the print that drove the entire 'the Fed must cut' conversation through August - was revised to a GAIN of 21,000. June was revised from 20,000 to 31,000. In the BLS's own words, June and July combined are 55,000 higher than previously reported. The contraction that anchored the dovish case did not happen. That is the sort of correction that rarely gets a headline and almost always matters more than the number that does.

The internals are softer than the headline, and being precise about that is worth more than either cheerleading or gloom. Average hourly earnings rose 0.3% on the month but DECELERATED to 3.1% year over year from 3.2% - so this was a volume beat, not a wage beat, and with July consumer inflation at 3.4% average wages are still not keeping pace with prices. Unemployment held at 4.1% and participation edged up to 61.6%. The broader U-6 underemployment rate improved to 7.7% from 7.9%, driven by 414,000 fewer people working part-time because they cannot find full-time work, which is genuinely good. But the job gains were concentrated in food services, at 59,000 against a 12,000 twelve-month average, and local government education at 42,000, while health care contributed only 13,000 against a 32,000 average and the information sector LOST 23,000 jobs. A strong headline built on restaurants and school hiring is not the same thing as a broad-based acceleration.

For clients: the useful way to hold this is that Friday did not settle the question, it moved it. Governor Christopher Waller said on Thursday, before the data, that he would be inclined to support holding rates if disinflation continued, that his decision 'will be heavily influenced by what we learn about August inflation,' and that he did not expect the employment data to deviate much from recent trend (Federal Reserve, speech text, September 3). He pre-committed to discounting exactly the report that then beat by a hundred thousand jobs. Markets took the hint: pricing for the September meeting rose only about three basis points on the day, which one rates strategist read as markets simply waiting for the inflation print (Bloomberg). Citigroup pushed its next-cut forecast all the way to June 2027 (Reuters). And the structural fact that reframes the whole year is unchanged: the futures market prices exactly ZERO probability of a rate CUT at any meeting through March 2027. Anyone still framing this cycle around when cuts arrive is anchored to a story the market abandoned months ago. The events that matter are the producer price report on Thursday, the consumer price report on Friday the 11th, and the Federal Reserve decision at 2:00 PM on September 16.
