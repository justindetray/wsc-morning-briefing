THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Wednesday's selloff was about interest rates, not earnings. Every Treasury yield from two years to thirty closed at its highest level of the year, the ten-year at 5.11%, and a five-year auction drew the weakest demand of 2026. Stocks fell 0.75%, which is an orderly response to a 15 basis point jump in the ten-year. For clients, the useful framing is that bonds are finally paying real money again, and the price of that is that stocks now have a competitor. Cintas beat and raised and still fell 3.4%, which is what a discount-rate day looks like. Today's test is the 7-year auction at 1:00 PM and the Trump-Xi bilateral.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The one-line version for clients this morning is that the bond market set the price of everything on Wednesday. Treasury's own curve file shows every maturity from two years to thirty closing at its highest level of 2026: the two-year at 4.85%, the five-year at 4.99%, the ten-year at 5.11% and the thirty-year at 5.40%. Those are clean new highs, not ties. The ten-year rose 15 basis points in a single session, and CNBC puts its intraday peak near 5.135%, the highest since July 2007. The trigger was a set of hot flash PMIs with input prices near a four-year high, and the Fed did nothing to push back: Governor Barr said further policy adjustments are likely to be needed and that the Fed had been out of position.

The five-year auction is the piece I would show a client who asks whether the bond market is working. It stopped at 5.033% with a bid-to-cover of 2.21, indirect bidders at 54.31% and dealers absorbing 15.77%. I pulled that from Treasury's auction record, and against the eight regular five-year auctions earlier this year it is the lowest bid-to-cover, the lowest indirect share and the largest dealer take. Dow Jones puts the tail at about 3.1 basis points. That is a poor auction, but it is not a failed one: the paper sold, at a price. The market is doing exactly what it is supposed to do, which is clear supply by offering a higher yield. The seven-year at 1:00 PM today is the next read.

Stocks behaved like a rates instrument. The S&P 500 fell 0.75% and the Nasdaq 100 fell 0.85%, so Tuesday's Nasdaq 100 record lasted one session and the index is now 0.85% below it; the S&P 500 is 1.19% below its August 13 record. The telling stock was Cintas, which beat on revenue and earnings, raised its full-year guidance, and fell 3.44%. That is not a verdict on Cintas. It is what happens to a highly valued, long-duration earnings stream when the rate used to discount it jumps. Paychex fell 8.77% on its own segment miss and McDonald's 4.81% on flat traffic. Volatility rose but stayed contained: the VIX closed at 15.18 and Nasdaq volatility rose less than the VIX did, which is the opposite of panic.

The rates repricing is spreading. CME FedWatch now has a 68.6% chance of a hike on October 28, up from 55.4% a day earlier, and 94.2% odds of at least one more hike by December. Overnight, Norway raised rates to 4.5% and Sweden signalled it will start hiking this year. In Tokyo, which reopened after a three-day holiday, the ten-year JGB yield touched 3.075%, its highest since 1996, and the Nikkei gained only 0.76% against the roughly 2.7% its futures had implied. When Japanese yields rise, the reason for Japanese savers to hold foreign bonds weakens at the margin, which is one more reason global long rates are moving together.

Oil is the other half of the inflation story. Brent settled at $103.08, up 3.86%, after Trump's Iran remarks at the UN, and the weekly government data showed crude stocks rising 3.0 million barrels rather than falling. So the move is geopolitical risk premium, not physical tightness, which also means it can reverse on a headline. On China, Treasury Secretary Bessent says the trade truce now runs to January 10 instead of November 10; Beijing has not confirmed it, and he said he did not know whether a bigger deal can be done. The Trump-Xi bilateral is today.

So the client framing is this. A 15 basis point day in the ten-year is large, but a 0.75% decline in stocks alongside it is orderly, and nothing in the auction or the volatility data looks like forced selling. The more durable point is that a ten-year at 5.11% and a two-year at 4.85% mean high-quality bonds now pay a meaningful income again, which is a genuine opportunity to lock in yield for clients who need it and a reason to be disciplined about what we pay for long-duration growth. What would change my view is a second weak auction today, with the seven-year tailing by more than two basis points, or a ten-year that keeps climbing toward 5.25% while the Fed talks about being out of position. That would turn an orderly repricing into a disorderly one.
