THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Nothing traded this weekend except crypto, and crypto went down: bitcoin is off about 1% this morning after giving back Saturday's gain. The real weekend story is that American and Chinese officials are in a room together today, on a Sunday, trying to build something Xi and Trump can sign on Thursday. The thing to watch this week is not a headline but three Treasury auctions - two-year Tuesday, five-year Wednesday, seven-year Thursday - because the entire move in yields last week was the market repricing the cost of money, not its inflation forecast. If those auctions clear badly, that is the story. If they clear fine, last week's selloff was positioning, not a verdict.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

Here is the honest state of play on a Sunday morning. US markets have not traded since Friday afternoon and do not reopen until 6:00 PM ET tonight, so every equity, bond, metal and energy number on the board is Friday's close. The only live market all weekend was crypto, where bitcoin rose Saturday and has given it back this morning, sitting near $80,300 and down about 1% on the day. Anyone quoting you a live oil or currency price right now is quoting a stale widget, not a trade.

What actually happened last week is worth restating because it sets up everything that follows. The Federal Reserve raised rates a quarter point to 3.75-4.00% on Wednesday on a unanimous vote, and the Bank of Japan raised to around 1.25% on Friday. The Dow had its worst week since March and its third consecutive weekly decline; the S&P fell for a second straight week; the Nasdaq finished higher on the strength of semiconductors. None of the four major US indexes is near a high - the S&P is about 1.9% below its August peak, the Nasdaq 3.3% below its June peak, and the semiconductor index still 18.5% below where it was in June.

The part that matters most is what drove the bond move. The two-year Treasury yield rose to 4.76% on Friday, the highest level of 2026, while the five- and ten-year yields matched but did not exceed the highs they set earlier in the week. Crucially, the ten-year inflation breakeven printed exactly 233 basis points on three consecutive sessions while the real, inflation-adjusted yield did all the moving. That is a specific and useful fact: investors are not raising their inflation expectations. They are raising the price they charge for lending money over time. A discount-rate repricing and an inflation scare look identical on a chart of nominal yields and call for completely different responses.

This week is built to test that. Treasury sells two-year notes Tuesday, five-year Wednesday and seven-year Thursday, with the ten-year sitting at 5%. There is almost no economic data - the personal consumption price index and the GDP revision are both the following week - so the auctions and the first Fed speeches since the communications blackout lifted will carry the week. Chicago's Goolsbee speaks Monday morning, Williams and Vice Chair Jefferson Tuesday morning, and Xi Jinping visits the White House Thursday. Futures now put better than even odds on another hike in October and roughly 90% on at least one more by December.

On the risk side, the Middle East got worse over the weekend rather than better. The Houthis fired a ballistic missile at Riyadh for the first time in this escalation, which Saudi Arabia says it intercepted, and claimed a strike on Aramco's facilities at Yanbu - the Red Sea terminus of the pipeline that is already damaged. Saudi Arabia says that attempt was thwarted too, and Aramco has said nothing at all. There was no pipeline update of any kind this weekend, which means last week's guidance of full capacity in about six weeks is now a week old. Crude settled Friday at $100.30 for West Texas and $103.87 for Brent, and diesel at the pump is at an all-time high of $6.4476 a gallon, which is the number that actually reaches households.

The advice does not change, and it should not. A repricing of the cost of capital is not a credit event and not a growth scare. Higher yields mark down the bonds you already own while raising the return on every dollar you invest from here, and they are the arithmetic reason the average stock has been lagging the index. Rebalancing into that gap is the opportunity. Reacting to the index level, or to a weekend headline about a market that will not open for another eleven hours, is the mistake.
