THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The call this morning is some version of "the 30-year Treasury just hit its highest level since 2007 and now the government is buying back its own bonds — is Washington losing control of interest rates?" The honest answer is that this is a real cost story and not a portfolio emergency. Long rates near 5.2% do make mortgages and business loans more expensive, which is a genuine household squeeze, but the long end rallied the moment Treasury stepped in, which is the opposite of a market losing confidence.

Two facts worth keeping: the S&P finished Wednesday slightly higher through all of it, and Japan's 30-year yield eased overnight instead of spiraling. There is nothing to do in your account today. What I am watching is September 9, when the larger buyback operations begin, and whether oil — up 3% overnight on the new Iran measures — holds these levels into month-end.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The mechanism here is narrower than the headlines suggest. Treasury raised the per-operation cap on its debt buyback program from $2 billion to at least $4 billion, covering the 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year sectors, for operations running September 9 through November 4 (Treasury press release, August 19). The buyback program itself is routine and has run since May 2024. What is unusual is the timing: the August 5 quarterly refunding had already set this quarter's liquidity-support budget, and Wednesday's move adds at least $14 billion more off-cycle. Against roughly $10 trillion of long-end debt outstanding that is small. It is a signal about intent, not a rescue.

It mattered because the long end had gotten disorderly. The 30-year yield touched roughly 5.3% earlier this week, its highest since June 2007, then fell about nine to ten basis points Wednesday after the announcement while the 10-year came in five to six. Japan's 30-year, which set a record earlier in the week, eased seven to eight basis points overnight to around 4.00%. This was a global long-end problem getting a US-shaped answer, and for one session the answer worked. Both gold and bitcoin ripped on it — gold up 4.1% Wednesday, bitcoin up 7.1% and another 3.7% overnight to its highest since early June, helped by a record $2.7 billion of crypto short liquidations.

Separately, the July FOMC minutes released Wednesday showed a 9-3 hold with three regional presidents dissenting in favor of a hike — Hammack, Kashkari and Logan — the first unified three-way same-direction dissent since 2016, with the minutes citing the Iran war as clouding the inflation outlook (Federal Reserve, August 19). Futures now price no cuts at all, and about a 31% chance of a hike in September, easing from 34% a day earlier (CME FedWatch, reporting date August 19). Overnight, oil added roughly 3% after the administration announced sweeping new economic measures against Iran, with the UAE suspending economic ties and reports of three China-linked supertankers turning back in the Strait of Hormuz.

Asia's chip complex staged a violent reversal that is worth reading correctly. KOSPI rose 5.89% and SK Hynix 12.73% after SK Hynix's board approved a 40 trillion won buyback and cancellation, roughly $28.6 billion, the largest ever by a listed Korean company, alongside a pledge to return more than half of free cash flow over three years (Aju Press, August 20). Samsung is reported to be readying a larger $71.75 billion return plan (Bloomberg, August 20). That is a capital-returns story, not a demand story, and the rebound was uneven — US-listed chip names had not caught the bounce as of the pre-open. For clients: the week's real question is not whether Washington can control long rates, but whether it needs to — and every move here is a market working loudly, not a market breaking.
