THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

A good week for stocks and a hard one for bonds, and the two are more connected than they look. The S&P 500 gained 1.2% and the Nasdaq 100 more than 3%, while the 30-year Treasury yield closed the week at its highest level of the year. Nearly all of the rise in yields came from real rates rather than inflation expectations, which means investors are demanding more to lend, not forecasting runaway prices. Late Friday, after markets closed, Iran's offer to reopen the Strait of Hormuz was reportedly rejected, so oil may open higher Sunday night. Nothing here calls for action; next week's inflation and jobs data will matter more than any headline.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

The simplest way to describe this week to a client: stocks went up, bonds went down, and both moves came from the same place - a stronger economy that is costing more to finance.

On the equity side, the S&P 500 rose 1.2% for the week to 7,743, the Nasdaq 100 rose 3.25%, and the Dow ended a three-week losing streak. Neither index is at a new high: the S&P is 0.71% below its August 13 record close and the Nasdaq 100 0.40% below its September 22 record. Friday's leaders were Microsoft, up 3.7% on new AI tools, and a broad lift as oil fell.

On the bond side, the 10-year Treasury yield rose 16 basis points for the week to 5.17%, and intraday Friday it touched its highest level since 2007 before easing. The 30-year closed at 5.49%, its highest of 2026. The point worth making to clients is what drove it: the inflation-protected 10-year yield rose 15 of those 16 basis points. The market's implied inflation forecast barely moved. Investors are simply charging more to lend money for ten years.

That fits Friday's data. Orders for business equipment jumped 1.6% in August, triple what economists expected - companies are investing, largely in AI and data centers. Consumers are the soft spot: Michigan sentiment finished September at 48.1, a four-month low, with households expecting 4.6% inflation over the next year. Strong business spending plus anxious households and $100 Brent is a mix that keeps the Fed leaning toward tightening. Futures give about a 64% chance of another hike in October and more than 90% by December, though those odds eased Friday.

The weekend risk is oil. Iran offered a seven-day plan to reopen the Strait of Hormuz, and the Wall Street Journal reports the President rejected it. That news arrived after Friday's settle, when Brent had already fallen 2.1% on hopes for a deal. Expect some of that decline to reverse when trading reopens Sunday evening.

The advice stays deliberately steady. Yields above 5% mean high-quality bonds now pay a real return again, which is an opportunity for anyone holding excess cash. Stocks are close to their highs without being euphoric - the VIX closed at 14.87. What would change the view: a 10-year close above 5.25%, or a hot August PCE report on Wednesday that pushes the market to price a third hike.
