Overnight

  • THE MEETING FRIDAY'S OIL REVERSAL WAS BOUGHT ON DID NOT HAPPEN. Oman's foreign minister confirmed that the ministerial on a new Strait of Hormuz maritime route is POSTPONED INDEFINITELY. No new date, nothing signed, nobody attended. The venue had been Salalah, not Muscat - Bloomberg had that right and Iran's president had it wrong on both the city and the claim that an agreement would be signed. Who asked for the delay is unreconciled three ways: Oman says consensus, Iran's Fars says some regional countries, and Iran's foreign-ministry spokesman said Monday it was Saudi Arabia. Saudi Arabia has not confirmed anything. Friday's market paid up for de-escalation and got a postponement with no calendar attached.
  • AND THE SAUDI EAST-WEST PIPELINE IS STILL SHUT. The Energy Ministry confirmed the line is down after a drone attack launched from Iraq. Yanbu has five to seven days of export cover, up to 4% of global supply is at risk, and repair estimates run from days to weeks with no official timeline. This is the line that bypasses Hormuz entirely, so it is the single most important unresolved physical fact in the market. Be careful with sourcing: a 'restored to 7 million barrels a day' headline is circulating and it is April 2026 news, not this weekend's. Brent is up 2.85% to $107.59 and WTI up 2.47% to $102.52 this morning, giving back Friday's entire de-escalation discount.
  • THE STRAIT IS PHYSICALLY TIGHTER, AND THE TRANSIT COUNT IS THE NUMBER TO WATCH. Hormuz transits have fallen to single digits per day against a 10-day average of 14, and that count excludes vessels going dark on AIS. Iran's Persian Gulf Strait Authority published a 77-vessel blacklist Monday, up from 45 on 24 August. UKMTO issued Warning 134-26 after a vessel was struck around 23:00 UTC Saturday, and a separate Iranian vessel incident left one dead and four wounded. The Houthis have reached Perim Island astride Bab el-Mandeb, which carries another 4 to 5% of global supply. There is no in-window tanker war-risk premium quote and no US naval force-movement announcement - if someone gives you either number today, they are making it up.
  • THE BIGGER EQUITY STORY IS NOT OIL - IT IS AN AI SLOWDOWN SHOCK. Dario Amodei published an essay Saturday urging the industry to deliberately slow the pace of frontier development, and it was publicly backed by Elon Musk and Sam Altman. Altman separately said OpenAI will not IPO in 2026. SoftBank fell between 10.7% and 11.7%. That is what is driving the futures split this morning: Nasdaq 100 futures are down 1.53% while S&P 500 futures are down only 0.64%. Korea's KOSPI fell 3.26%. The Nikkei closed down 0.81% at 63,492.99, its lowest close since 30 July, while the Topix ROSE 0.74% to 4,058.21 - a split, not a contradiction, because Japanese banks rallied on BoJ hike pricing and Nikkei breadth was actually positive at 160 advancers to 64.
  • RATES AND FX ARE DOING SOMETHING THE HIKE NARRATIVE DOES NOT EXPLAIN. The yen WEAKENED to 154.35 from 153.58 even as the market prices a roughly 79% chance of a BoJ hike Friday, with the 10-year JGB at 2.982%. Sterling is at a one-month low near 1.3486, the euro is 1.1553, and the dollar index is up 0.31%. Gilts are at multi-year highs into Thursday's Bank of England decision. Gold is DOWN 1.65% to $4,336 despite the geopolitical escalation, which is the tell that this is a real-rates move rather than a fear move. Bitcoin is up 1.26% to $77,777. Friday's Treasury curve had the 30-year at 5.35%, tying June 2007, with the 20-year at 5.38% - still inverted at the long end.
  • THE FED MEETS WEDNESDAY, THE STREET FLIPPED OVER THE WEEKEND, AND THE ECONOMISTS HAVE NOT. Goldman Sachs and J.P. Reuters' 9 September economist poll still says hold through year-end. That is the live disagreement into Wednesday. The VIX closed Friday at 15.84 after falling 11.2%; it is indicated near 17.5 this morning, but that is an unsettled pre-market print, not a level.
THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

Friday's rally had one premise: that Monday's meeting in Oman would start reopening the Strait of Hormuz. This morning Oman confirmed the meeting is postponed indefinitely - no new date, nothing signed, nobody there. The Saudi East-West pipeline that bypasses Hormuz is still shut with five to seven days of export cover at Yanbu, and strait transits have fallen to single digits a day. Crude took Friday's discount straight back: Brent +2.9% to $107.59. But the sharper move this morning is in tech, not energy - Nasdaq futures are down 1.53% against the S&P's 0.64% because the AI industry spent the weekend publicly debating whether to slow itself down. Diesel set an eighth straight record at $6.23. Wednesday the Fed is being asked to hike into a supply shock it cannot influence.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

WHAT ACTUALLY CHANGED OVERNIGHT. Two things, and they point in different directions.

First, the diplomatic premise behind Friday's oil reversal is gone. Friday, Brent settled at $104.61 after trading as high as $110.19, and the S&P rose 0.86% while the VIX fell 11% - all of it bought on the idea that a ministerial meeting in Oman on Monday would begin negotiating safe passage through the Strait of Hormuz. This morning Oman's foreign minister confirmed the meeting is postponed indefinitely. No new date. Nothing signed. Nobody attended. Even the venue was misreported by the Iranian side. And who asked for the delay is still unreconciled three ways, with Iran's foreign ministry pointing at Saudi Arabia and Saudi Arabia saying nothing. Crude has taken the discount back: Brent is up 2.85% to $107.59 and WTI up 2.47% to $102.52.

More important than the meeting: the Saudi East-West pipeline is still shut after a drone attack launched from Iraq. That line is the one that moves crude to the Red Sea without going through Hormuz at all. Yanbu has five to seven days of export cover, up to 4% of global supply is at risk, and the repair window is officially 'days to weeks' with no timeline. Meanwhile strait transits have dropped to single digits a day against a ten-day average of fourteen, and Iran expanded its vessel blacklist from 45 names to 77. The transit count is the honest indicator here, not the headline crude price.

SECOND, AND THIS IS THE PART MOST COMMENTARY WILL MISS THIS MORNING: the sharper move is in technology, and it has nothing to do with oil. Nasdaq 100 futures are down 1.53% while S&P 500 futures are down only 0.64%. Korea fell 3.26%. SoftBank dropped roughly 11%. The cause is that Dario Amodei of Anthropic published an essay Saturday arguing the industry should deliberately slow the pace of frontier AI development, and Elon Musk and Sam Altman publicly agreed with him. Altman also said OpenAI will not go public in 2026. For a market whose leadership has been almost entirely an AI capex story, the people building it saying 'perhaps slower' is a genuine repricing event.

WHAT TO TELL A CLIENT WHO CALLS THIS MORNING. Start with the fact that this is two separate stories and neither one is a reason to change a plan. The energy story is a supply disruption with a physical fix - a pipeline gets repaired, a strait gets transited - and the market has repriced it twice in three sessions in both directions, which tells you nobody has information, only positioning. The AI story is more interesting and more durable: if the pace of frontier development genuinely slows, that changes the shape of the capex cycle that has carried index returns for three years. It does not mean the technology stops mattering. It means the concentration risk in a cap-weighted index is being reminded of itself, which is an argument for the diversification we already own rather than an argument to do something today.

Second thing worth saying out loud: nothing on this page is at a record. That matters because the tape feels stronger than it is. The S&P is 1.8% below its 13 August closing high, the Nasdaq 100 is 4.2% below its 2 June high, the Dow is 3.3% off its 5 August high, and the semiconductor index is 19.2% below where it closed on 22 June. Friday was a good day inside a market that has been going sideways-to-down for a month.

WEDNESDAY IS THE REAL EVENT AND THE CONVERSATION HAS INVERTED. The federal funds target range is 3.50-3.75% and the question is whether the Fed RAISES it, not whether it cuts. Three members already dissented in July in favour of a hike. August core CPI ran +0.3% month over month with headline +0.4% and the year-over-year rate at 3.4%, and the BLS said gasoline alone accounted for more than a third of the monthly increase. Our own read of CME pricing off the 11 September cohort puts a quarter-point hike at 87.5% Wednesday, a hold at 12.5%, and a cut at zero, with a 97.7% cumulative chance of at least one hike by December. Goldman Sachs and J.P. Morgan both flipped to a hike over the weekend - though Goldman said plainly that it flipped on market pricing rather than its own forecast, which makes it a follower, not a signal. Reuters' economist poll from 9 September still says hold through year-end. That disagreement is the trade.

THE UNCOMFORTABLE PART. The Fed is being asked to tighten into a supply shock it cannot influence. Higher rates do not repair a Saudi pipeline or reopen a strait. What they do is slow demand at home to offset a price increase imposed from abroad - and they would be doing it with consumer sentiment already down to 47.8 from 51.7 and one-year inflation expectations up 60 basis points to 4.6%. If I could keep one number this morning it would be diesel: the national average hit $6.2301 today, an eighth consecutive record and 69% higher than a year ago, while mid-grade and premium gasoline actually FELL day over day. That divergence is a distillate crack story, not a consumer-demand story, and diesel is an input to nearly everything that moves, so it propagates into goods prices with a lag no rate decision can outrun. The long end already sees it: the 30-year is 5.35%, tying June 2007, and the 20-year yields more than the 30-year, which is not a normal market.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND. Three things, in order. One: a verified restart of the Saudi East-West pipeline from the Ministry of Energy or Aramco - that is worth more than any communique and would take the 4%-of-supply tail off the table. Two: Hormuz transit counts recovering back toward the ten-day average of fourteen, which would tell us commercial operators believe the risk has passed regardless of what ministers say. Three: the Fed holding Wednesday with a dot plot showing no further hikes, which would mean the Committee is willing to look through an oil-driven headline print. Until then the posture is the boring one, and it is the right one: stay invested, stay diversified, own some real assets, and treat a week with a Fed decision, a Bank of Japan decision and quad witching as a source of opportunity rather than a reason to act.
