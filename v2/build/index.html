THE SHORT ANSWER · IF A CLIENT CALLS

The question this weekend is "bitcoin is up 24% in a week and gold is up 5%, so why is my stock account down?" Two sides of one story. Friday's business survey showed the fastest output growth in over four years, and strong growth with inflation above 3% means the Fed's next move is likelier a hike than a cut. Futures price a 42% chance of a hike in September and no cut at any meeting into next year.

Repricing what money costs marks down anything valued on distant cash flows, which is why chips were worst, off 5% on the week, while hard assets caught the bid. The part that reaches your household is gasoline, and that traces to oil and the Strait of Hormuz, not to your portfolio. A 1.4% down week is noise and nothing in the plan changes; I am watching Wednesday, when the Fed's inflation gauge lands the same day Nvidia reports, and Friday, when the Fed chair speaks at Jackson Hole.

THE FULLER PICTURE · IF THEY WANT THE MECHANICS

Start with what actually happened Friday, because it explains the whole week. S&P Global's flash August composite came in at 56.0 against about 54.0 expected and 54.5 in July, with services at 56.8 against 54.0 expected - the fastest output growth in more than four years. Manufacturing was the lone soft spot at 53.2, slightly missing. Stocks took it well on the day; the Dow rose 518 points and recovered roughly three quarters of Thursday's Walmart-driven decline. The rates market did not. Ten-year yields rose three basis points to 4.74%, testing a 20-month high, the two-year rose nearly four to 4.24%, and the odds of a September rate hike climbed to 41.9% from 38.3% Thursday and 31% Wednesday, with the probability of a cut at any meeting through January 2027 still exactly zero (CME FedWatch, reporting date August 21). Growth is not the problem. The price of money is.

That is also the cleanest read on why hard assets ran while equities fell. Treasury said this week it would at least double its long-dated buybacks, to $4 billion per operation, in an explicit attempt to hold long yields down - and by Friday the ten-year had more than erased the entire decline that followed the announcement. Wire coverage genuinely disagrees on this point, with some outlets framing Friday as bond-market stabilization and the Wall Street Journal noting yields held near decade highs despite Treasury's efforts; the tape sides with the Journal. Against a government trying to cap its own borrowing costs, gold rose about 5% on the week to $4,607 and bitcoin rose 24.4%, its largest weekly gain in the full 104 weeks of daily history checked. Two things worth keeping straight: wires disagree on whether bitcoin's prior comparable week was March 2023 or March 2024, and despite the move it remains 37% below its October 2025 record, while gold is 16% below its January record. Meanwhile the ten-year breakeven sits at an ordinary 2.36%. This is a repricing of real yields and term premium, not a market forecasting an inflation spiral.

The week's real damage was in semiconductors, and it is worth separating from the index. The Philadelphia semiconductor index fell 5.4% on the week and fell again Friday while the Dow rose, leaving it 19.8% below its June 22 record. Honest caveat: I could not find a properly sourced explanation for Friday's specific chip weakness - the only thing available was a Barron's headline about China competition concerns with no supporting article, so treat the cause as unconfirmed. The more likely mechanic is positioning ahead of Wednesday, when NVIDIA reports against its own guidance of $91 billion plus or minus 2%, on the same day the July PCE price index lands at 8:30 in the morning. Friday was also monthly options expiration, which mechanically releases some dealer hedging pressure into next week; no reputable desk published a notional or gamma figure for this specific expiration, so I am not going to invent one.

The geopolitical item is the one with a household cost attached. Reuters reported Saturday that oil shipments through the Strait of Hormuz have virtually halted, with only four commodity ships transiting on Thursday and none of them large crude or LNG carriers, as Iran threatens unauthorized tankers; Iran did grant transit to a number of Iraqi tankers after requests from Baghdad, a small easing. US Central Command says American forces have redirected 68 commercial vessels. Brent settled at $94.39 and WTI at $87.06, a second consecutive weekly gain of roughly 5%, and Secretary Bessent details the new sanctions Monday afternoon. Separately, Reuters columnists note that Chair Warsh has pointed to balance-sheet reduction and a productivity boom as alternatives to raising rates, which is the live tension going into Friday: futures price a 42% chance of a September hike from a Chair who has signalled hikes are not his preferred tool. For clients: this was a week that repriced the cost of money rather than the health of the economy, and the higher real yields that make portfolios uncomfortable today are the same higher real yields that raise the return on every dollar invested from here - which is why the honest answer to almost every question this weekend is that the plan does not change.
